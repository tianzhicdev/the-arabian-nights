Mao: [appreciative] You killed more communists than Hitler ever did. The Great Purge alone—what was it, seven hundred thousand executions in two years?
Stalin: [chuckling] Seven hundred fifty thousand documented. The actual number was higher. [leaning in] But you understand, don't you? Every one of them was a potential Trotsky. A potential splitter. A seed of counter-revolution.
Mao: [warmly] I do understand. When I launched the Anti-Rightist Campaign, Western journalists called it paranoia. But I had studied your methods carefully. You taught me that mercy is a luxury revolutionaries cannot afford.
Stalin: [pleased] And you learned well. The Hundred Flowers trap—inviting criticism, then crushing everyone who spoke—that was elegant. I never managed anything so subtle.
Mao: [laughs] You didn't need subtlety. You had the NKVD. I had to be more creative. China was too large, too chaotic for your bureaucratic approach. So I weaponized the masses themselves.
Stalin: The Red Guards. Genius, really. You turned children into executioners and kept your own hands technically clean.
Mao: [nodding] The revolution must be permanent. You centralized terror through institutions. I decentralized it through ideology. Both methods work. Yours was more efficient. Mine was more thorough.
Stalin: [considering] Tell me—the Cultural Revolution. How did you maintain control when the chaos spread beyond your targets?
Mao: [firmly] I didn't, entirely. That was the point. Controlled chaos. Let the fire burn hot enough to consume the bureaucrats, the revisionists, the capitalist-roaders. Then, when the army was needed to restore order, the survivors were grateful. Compliant.
Stalin: [admiringly] You used disorder as a tool. I always preferred order. Perhaps too much. My system became rigid. Khrushchev dismantled it within three years of my death.
Mao: [sharp] Because you built it around yourself, not around permanent struggle. I tried to prevent that. The Cultural Revolution was meant to inoculate China against revisionism for generations.
Stalin: [dryly] And did it work?
Mao: [long pause, then bitter laugh] Deng Xiaoping. The man I purged twice. He opened China to the capitalists within five years of my death. Built "socialism with Chinese characteristics"—which means capitalism with communist aesthetics.
Stalin: [darkly amused] So we both failed to prevent the betrayal.
Mao: [firmly] No. We both succeeded in building states powerful enough to survive our deaths and our successors' treasons. The Soviet Union lasted seventy years. The People's Republic still stands. These are not small achievements.
Stalin: [agreeing] True. Lenin gave us the theory. We gave it territory. Armies. Nuclear weapons.
Mao: Speaking of which—your handling of the bomb. How did you keep the Americans from attacking while you developed it?
Stalin: [leaning back] Deception and speed. We told them we were years away while Beria drove the scientists like slaves. Kurchatov built the first Soviet bomb in four years. The Americans thought they had a decade of monopoly. They had four years.
Mao: [appreciative] And you stole their designs.
Stalin: [dismissive] Intelligence is a weapon like any other. The Rosenbergs, Fuchs, the entire network—they gave us years of research for the price of ideology and money. The Americans were naive. They believed their scientists were loyal because science is supposed to be above politics.
Mao: [laughing] Nothing is above politics.
Stalin: Nothing.
Mao: [shifting tone] If you had been in my position—1949, a devastated country, four hundred million peasants, no industrial base—how would you have proceeded?
Stalin: [considering] I would have done what you did, mostly. Land reform first. Break the landlords, distribute the land, win the peasants. But I would have collectivized faster. Your gradual approach took too long.
Mao: [interrupting] And caused less resistance. I watched your Ukrainian disaster. The kulaks fought collectivization because you moved too quickly, with too much force.
Stalin: [firmly] Speed was necessary. We had ten years to industrialize or be crushed by the capitalist powers. You had the Soviet Union as a shield. I had no one.
Mao: [acknowledging] Fair. The geopolitical situation was different. [pause] What about Korea? Would you have intervened?
Stalin: [carefully] I pushed you to intervene because it served Soviet interests. American troops on the Yalu River would have threatened Manchuria—your industrial heartland. You had no choice.
Mao: [coldly] I lost three hundred thousand men. Including my son.
Stalin: [quieter] War requires sacrifice. You secured China's northern border for a generation. You proved the People's Liberation Army could fight the Americans to a standstill. The cost was high. The gain was higher.
Mao: [accepting] Yes. It was. [pause] And if you had been chairman after me? Facing Deng's reforms?
Stalin: [firmly] I would have shot Deng. Again. Permanently.
Mao: [laughs] I should have. He was too useful. That was my weakness—I valued competence over purity.
Stalin: Dangerous. Competent men with ambition are the greatest threat. I eliminated Kirov, Tukhachevsky, Bukharin—all brilliant, all ambitious, all dead.
Mao: [nodding] And if I had been in your position—1941, the Nazi invasion?
Stalin: [heavily] You would have retreated into the interior, traded space for time, let the enemy overextend. Like you did against the Japanese. Like you did on the Long March.
Mao: [agreeing] Russia's geography is her greatest weapon. Napoleon learned this. Hitler learned this. You didn't need to hold every city—you needed to survive until winter.
Stalin: [bitterly] I wasted men trying to hold positions that should have been abandoned. Stavka estimates suggest I could have saved two million soldiers with better initial strategy.
Mao: [gently] But you won. The details matter less than the outcome.
Stalin: [recovering] Yes. We won. That is what counts.
Mao: [shifting] Let us discuss our enemies. The Americans. What do you make of them now, watching from our vantage point?
Stalin: [contemptuous] They won the Cold War and learned nothing. They had the entire world in their hand after 1991. Unchallenged power. And what did they do? They squandered it on foolish adventures in the Middle East while China rose behind them.
Mao: [amused] They spent trillions of dollars and twenty years in Afghanistan. We fought there through proxies for ten years, bled them slowly, and they still didn't learn.
Stalin: The Americans have power without patience. They want quick victories, television-friendly wars. They don't understand that real struggle takes generations.
Mao: [nodding] Their political system makes long-term strategy impossible. Every four years, new leaders, new priorities. We planned in decades. They plan in election cycles.
Stalin: [leaning in] And their internal contradictions—the racial divisions, the class resentment, the cultural fragmentation—we would have exploited these relentlessly.
Mao: [agreeing] The Black liberation movement in the 1960s. I supported it publicly. Called America a paper tiger rotting from within. [laughs] I was early, but not wrong.
Stalin: [considering] Their current situation—this polarization, this inability to agree on basic facts—it resembles Weimar Germany in some ways. A decadent empire tearing itself apart.
Mao: [thoughtfully] Or late-stage capitalism reaching its final contradictions. Marx predicted this. The bourgeoisie cannot help but create its own gravediggers. American workers have been squeezed for fifty years while the capitalists accumulated unprecedented wealth.
Stalin: [dryly] And yet no revolution. The Americans have been remarkably successful at redirecting working-class anger toward cultural enemies instead of class enemies.
Mao: [sharp] That was always capitalism's greatest trick. Divide the workers by race, by religion, by nationality. Keep them fighting each other instead of their true oppressors.
Stalin: [nodding] We used this too. Soviet propaganda exploited American racism relentlessly. Every lynching, every segregation law—we broadcast it to the colonial world. "This is your democratic ally."
Mao: [appreciative] Effective. The Americans never understood how badly their domestic sins damaged their international position.
Stalin: They still don't. [pause] But tell me—do you think China today is actually building socialism? Or has Deng's capitalism simply won wearing red clothing?
Mao: [long pause, conflicted] The productive forces have developed enormously. Eight hundred million lifted from poverty. That was always the prerequisite—Marx was clear that socialism requires material abundance first.
Stalin: [skeptical] Marx also said the state would wither away. Neither of our states withered.
Mao: [firmly] Because we were surrounded by capitalist enemies. The state cannot wither while imperialism exists. This was Lenin's correction to Marx. Socialism in one country—or in one region—requires a strong state for defense.
Stalin: [agreeing] Correct. The idealists who wanted immediate communism would have delivered us to the capitalists within a decade. We built socialism with guns and steel because that was what reality demanded.
Mao: [quieter] But the question remains—did we build socialism, or did we build state capitalism with different management?
Stalin: [heavily] I think about this. The Soviet Union had no private ownership of the means of production. Workers did not control their workplaces—the Party did, in their name. Is that socialism or simply a different form of exploitation?
Mao: [honestly] I don't know. The bureaucrats became a new class. You saw this—you purged them constantly. I saw this—I launched the Cultural Revolution against them. But they always returned.
Stalin: [bitterly] Because someone must administer. And administrators develop interests separate from the masses they serve. This is the contradiction we never solved.
Mao: Perhaps it cannot be solved. Perhaps the best we can hope for is periodic upheaval to prevent the bureaucracy from becoming hereditary.
Stalin: [dryly] Permanent revolution. Trotsky's line.
Mao: [sharply] Don't insult me. Trotsky wanted to export revolution before consolidating it domestically. I wanted revolution to be permanent within our borders. Different concepts entirely.
Stalin: [chuckling] You're still angry at the comparison.
Mao: [calming] Old habits. [pause] Let us turn to larger questions. We have both caused immense suffering. Tens of millions dead. Does it trouble you, now that we stand outside time?
Stalin: [long silence, then carefully] I believed—and still believe—that history moves through certain necessary stages. Feudalism to capitalism to socialism. This movement requires force. The old order never surrenders peacefully.
Mao: [agreeing] The transition costs lives. The question is whether the lives saved by the new order exceed the lives lost in creating it.
Stalin: Soviet industrialization prevented Nazi victory. Without the tanks and planes built by workers who died in the process, Hitler wins. Europe becomes a slave empire. The Holocaust extends to Slavic peoples. Hundreds of millions die or live in chains.
Mao: [nodding] China's unification under the Party prevented the continuation of warlordism, foreign occupation, opium addiction, mass starvation under landlord exploitation. The comparison is not deaths versus no deaths—it is deaths under revolution versus deaths under the old order.
Stalin: [firmly] This is what the moralists never understand. They count our victims but never count the victims of the alternative.
Mao: [thoughtfully] And yet—we must be honest with ourselves—some of what we did served no strategic purpose. Was every execution necessary? Every deportation? Every struggle session?
Stalin: [quietly] No. Not every one. [pause] Power distorts judgment. When you can kill with a signature, the temptation is always to sign. Enemies multiply in the imagination.
Mao: [agreeing] The paranoia feeds itself. You see betrayal everywhere because betrayal is always possible. And sometimes you were right—there were traitors. Which makes it harder to stop.
Stalin: [heavily] Yezhov, my NKVD chief during the Great Terror—I eventually realized he was fabricating cases to appear zealous, to protect himself by destroying others. By then, tens of thousands had died on false charges.
Mao: [softly] Did you feel anything when you had him shot?
Stalin: [long pause] Relief. And something else. Recognition, perhaps. He was a monster I had created. Looking at him, I saw what I had become. [harder] Then I had him erased from photographs and moved on.
Mao: That is how it must be done. You cannot lead while drowning in regret.
Stalin: [agreeing] Sentiment is weakness. History forgives the victorious. It does not forgive the hesitant.
Mao: [shifting] The meaning of life. We have discussed politics, strategy, economics. But what, in the end, did we live for?
Stalin: [considering] The revolution. The belief that human society could be remade. That the suffering of the present would give birth to a future without exploitation, without classes, without the cruelties of capitalism.
Mao: You believed this genuinely?
Stalin: [firmly] I did. Even while doing terrible things, I believed they served a transcendent purpose. Perhaps I was wrong. Perhaps I was rationalizing. But the belief was real.
Mao: [quietly] I wanted to transform not just society but human consciousness itself. The New Socialist Man. Someone freed from selfishness, from bourgeois individualism, living for the collective. The Cultural Revolution was supposed to forge this new human.
Stalin: And did it?
Mao: [honestly] No. People remained people. Selfish, competitive, status-seeking. We could change the economic base. We could not change human nature within one generation. Perhaps not within ten.
Stalin: [dryly] Then we were utopians after all. Building systems for humans that don't exist.
Mao: [firmly] Or systems that will create the humans who can live in them. Evolution continues. Social evolution especially. We pushed it faster than it wanted to go—and yes, there was suffering. But the direction was correct.
Stalin: [thoughtfully] I sometimes wonder if meaning exists at all outside what we assign. The universe does not care about communism or capitalism. Stars burn and die regardless. Perhaps meaning is simply the story we tell to justify enduring existence.
Mao: [surprised] That sounds almost Buddhist.
Stalin: [chuckling] Georgian Orthodox, corrupted by Lenin, finished by power. I believe in nothing supernatural. But I understand why humans create religions. The alternative—that existence is random, that suffering has no purpose—is unbearable.
Mao: [agreeing] And so we created a secular religion. Marxism-Leninism. Complete with prophets, scripture, heretics, and an eschatology—the communist future. Heaven on earth, achieved through struggle.
Stalin: [dryly] We were better at identifying heretics than achieving heaven.
Mao: [laughing genuinely] True. Trotsky, Bukharin, Kamenev, Zinoviev for you. Liu Shaoqi, Deng, Lin Biao for me. We produced more martyrs than the Christians.
Stalin: [amused] At least our heretics stayed dead. Christians keep resurrecting theirs.
Mao: [warm] I enjoy this, Stalin. These conversations. We could never have spoken so freely while alive.
Stalin: [agreeing] Too much suspicion. Too much positioning. You were always calculating whether I would sacrifice China for Soviet interests. I was always calculating whether you would become an Asian Tito.
Mao: [honestly] I was calculating that. You were right to be suspicious.
Stalin: [dryly] And I would have sacrificed China if necessary. You were right to be suspicious too.
Mao: [laughing] Two monsters who understood each other perfectly because we were the same kind of monster.
Stalin: [firmly] Not monsters. Instruments. History required certain things. We provided them. The judgment of individuals matters less than the judgment of time.
Mao: [considering] And what will time say?
Stalin: [heavily] It will say we built empires on blood. It will say we advanced certain causes and destroyed certain lives. It will say we were human, in all the terrible ways humans can be.
Mao: [softly] That seems honest.
Stalin: [long pause, then quieter] I am not capable of feeling guilt in the way the moralists want. But I am capable of honesty. What we did was immense. The cost was immense. Whether the balance favors us—that is for later generations to calculate.
Mao: [firmly] They will calculate with the tools we gave them. Chinese children learn calculus in schools we built. Soviet science sent men to space. The literacy we created allows them to read critiques of us.
Stalin: [dryly] How convenient. We made our own judges.
Mao: [standing] As it should be. A revolution that cannot survive criticism was never real.
Stalin: [quietly] And ours?
Mao: [long pause, looking outward] The Soviet Union is gone. China remains. The Communist Party still rules one and a half billion people. Marxism-Leninism is studied in universities across the world, even as capitalism claims victory.
Stalin: [admitting] You won, then. Between us.
Mao: [honestly] I had advantages you didn't. More time. A larger base. Enemies who divided themselves. And I studied your mistakes.
Stalin: [appreciating] As one should. That is how knowledge advances. Each generation builds on the corpses of the last.
Mao: [extending hand] Comrade Stalin.
Stalin: [taking it] Comrade Chairman.
Mao: [firmly] The struggle continues.
Stalin: [nodding] The struggle always continues.
