Nietzsche: [settling back in] The dinner party.
Chernyshevsky: [nodding, preparing to tell the story] The dinner party. [thoughtful pause] After the episode with the officer, the Underground Man retreats into what he calls his "dreams." [explaining] Not plans, not hopes—dreams. Fantasies of heroism, of love, of becoming a great benefactor of mankind. [quoting] "I was a poet and a grand gentleman, and I fell in love... I triumphed over everyone; everyone, of course, was in dust and ashes, and was forced spontaneously to recognize my superiority, and I forgave them all."
Nietzsche: [understanding] The imagination compensating for the paralysis.
Chernyshevsky: [agreeing] For three months at a time he would retreat into these fantasies—then emerge feeling disgusted, craving what he calls "debauchery." [wry] Though even his debauchery was solitary and secret. [quoting] "I indulged in solitary, nocturnal dissipation of the most degraded sort, with feelings of shame that never left me even in the worst moments."
Nietzsche: [curious] Who is Zverkov?
Chernyshevsky: [with distaste] Everything the Underground Man is not. [explaining] Handsome. Confident. Good with women. Popular. Wealthy. The son of a landowner with serfs. [quoting] "He was a pretty but stupid fellow... but while we were at school together, we all thought him a fine fellow... He was vulgar in the extreme, but at the same time he was a good-natured fellow, even in his vulgarity."
Nietzsche: [understanding] And he is invited to this dinner?
Chernyshevsky: [emphatic] No! That is exactly the point. [explaining] He forces himself into it. He overhears the planning—dinner at five o'clock at the Hôtel de Paris, seven rubles per head—and he invites himself. [quoting] "All at once—I don't know what moved me—but I took it into my head to intrude upon this dinner, though I knew that by so doing I should certainly be unwelcome and that it would be tactless and absolutely uncalled for."
Chernyshevsky: [new section, building tension] The night before the dinner, he cannot sleep. [describing] He constructs elaborate fantasies. Either he will dazzle them with his brilliance—they will see his true superiority, they will crowd around him in admiration—or he will deliberately humiliate Zverkov, expose him as a fool, achieve some kind of moral victory.
Nietzsche: [quietly] He is already preparing to fail.
Chernyshevsky: [agreeing] The morning of the dinner, he sends his servant Apollon to borrow money for the meal. [explaining] Apollon is a strange character—a tailor who has stayed on as the Underground Man's servant, reading psalms aloud, looking down on his employer with unconcealed contempt. [quoting] "An elderly man of great dignity, who worked part-time as a tailor. But for some unknown reason he despised me, even beyond all measure, and looked down upon me insufferably."
Nietzsche: [heavily] An hour of anticipation. Of rehearsal.
Chernyshevsky: [describing] He paces. He sits. He imagines the conversations he will have. His anxiety builds. [quoting] "I was overwhelmed with confusion and felt most humiliated... The thought that I was an uninvited guest, that they would hate me, that they would despise me, haunted me."
Chernyshevsky: [building the disaster, storytelling mode] At first, Zverkov is gracious—almost patronizing. He speaks to the Underground Man with the kind of easy courtesy that feels worse than contempt. [quoting] "'You've been a stranger to us, Dobroluybov,' he said, quite familiarly and almost condescendingly—I did not even know my name properly. But I may observe that though he greeted me quite graciously, even with condescension, there was a shade of triumphant insolence, as though by greeting me so he were showing off his impregnable superiority."
Nietzsche: [noting] He feels the condescension even in kindness.
Chernyshevsky: [continuing] The conversation excludes him. They talk about women—Zverkov's conquests, his prospects in the provinces. They talk about promotions, about mutual acquaintances. [describing] Whenever the Underground Man tries to participate, his remarks fall flat. [quoting] "I purposely tried to join in, but every word I uttered was so out of keeping with what they were saying that they simply stared at me in astonishment and then went on talking as though I had not spoken."
Nietzsche: [quietly] The alcohol loosening the spite.
Chernyshevsky: [escalating] He makes cutting remarks that no one acknowledges. He contradicts Zverkov on trivial points. He announces loudly that he despises "ready-made phrases" and "commonplace talk"—a clear insult to everyone at the table. [quoting] "All stared at me in amazement, and for a moment there was silence."
Nietzsche: [grim] He cannot even insult honestly.
Chernyshevsky: [continuing] The table falls silent. Then Zverkov laughs nervously. [quoting] "'That's quite out of place,' Trudolyubov observed, 'and you really ought not to have said such stupid things.'" The Underground Man demands to know what he means. The situation escalates. [building] He accuses Zverkov of insulting him at school—some comment about his family, years ago. Zverkov barely remembers.
Chernyshevsky: [new section, describing the nadir] What follows is the lowest point of his life. [heavy] They ignore him completely. They move to a sofa in the corner and continue their conversation. He is left alone at the table.
Nietzsche: [quietly] Three hours.
Chernyshevsky: [emphatic] Three hours! [continuing] He paces. They drink. Occasionally one of them glances at him and laughs. [quoting] "They paid no attention whatever to me, though I intentionally made a noise with my chair... passed close to them, walked up and down in front of them." [desperate] He hopes they will notice his suffering. He hopes they will feel guilty. He hopes for any acknowledgment that he exists.
Nietzsche: [slowly] He is invisible.
Chernyshevsky: [agreeing, heavy] Completely invisible. [continuing] At eleven o'clock, they prepare to leave—for a brothel. Olympia's. They do not invite him. [describing] He approaches Simonov, the one quasi-friend, and demands—in front of everyone—to borrow six rubles.
Chernyshevsky: [new section, the chase] They leave. He follows. [describing the cab ride, building] Racing through the wet snow, alone, he oscillates between fantasies of triumph and clear-eyed recognition of their absurdity.
Nietzsche: [darkly] The theater of humiliation.
Chernyshevsky: [continuing] He knows these fantasies are absurd. [quoting] "Of course, all this was so stupid, so stupid that it could not possibly come to pass." But he cannot stop. The cab races through the snow.
Nietzsche: [quietly] The chase ends in an empty room.
Chernyshevsky: [heavily] Not quite empty. [significant pause] The madam sends a girl to him. A young woman, twenty years old, new to the profession. Her name is Liza.
Nietzsche: [after a long silence] He could have stopped at any point. Left the dinner. Not followed them. Not borrowed the money.
Chernyshevsky: [agreeing] At every step, he chose to continue. [quoting] "The more tactless, the more unseemly my going would be, the more certainly I would go."
Nietzsche: [philosophical] This is not merely social awkwardness. This is will—turned against itself. The determination to fail. To prove something through failure that success could never demonstrate.
Chernyshevsky: [challenging] And what is that?
Nietzsche: [slowly] That he exists. That he cannot be overlooked. That even if they will not acknowledge him as an equal, they must acknowledge him as a presence—an irritant, a wound, a conscience. [intense] The pacing for three hours is not weakness. It is a kind of terrible strength. He forces them to be aware of what they are doing to him—even as they refuse to care.
Chernyshevsky: [troubled] You admire this?
Nietzsche: [carefully] I recognize it. [pause] The man who cannot win on the field of action retreats to the field of consciousness. He makes his suffering into an accusation. [darker] It is the strategy of the powerless throughout history. The slave's revenge on the master.
Chernyshevsky: [pushing back] But it accomplishes nothing! He changes nothing. He gains nothing. He only deepens his own misery.
Nietzsche: [agreeing but qualifying] True. [thoughtful] But Dostoevsky is not showing us how to live. He is showing us what happens when life becomes impossible. [pointed] And that, Nikolai, may be the most devastating critique of your Crystal Palace. Not that it cannot be built—but that it will never accommodate the Underground Man. And there will always be Underground Men.
Chernyshevsky: [firmly] Because society produces them! Change the society—
Nietzsche: [interrupting, intense] And you will produce different forms of the same disease. [explaining] The need to matter, the rage at being overlooked, the preference for suffering over insignificance—these are not accidents of history. They are conditions of consciousness itself.
Chernyshevsky: [after a pause, quieter] Perhaps. [looking at the fire] But wait until you hear what happens with Liza. Wait until you see what he does when someone actually reaches for him.
Nietzsche: [settling back] I am waiting.
