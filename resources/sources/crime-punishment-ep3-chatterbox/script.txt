# Narrated by Celeste

# ============================================================

"It's my property!" the peasant screams.

And the axe falls again and again on the mare's skull.

And a boy—seven years old—breaks free from his father's hand and runs to kiss the horse's bleeding face.

[pause-2]

Raskolnikov wakes gasping in the bushes.

"Good God! Can it be that I shall really take an axe?"

[pause-5]

# ============================================================
# INTRODUCTION
# ============================================================

This is Celeste, telling you the story from Crime and Punishment.

[pause-3]

# ============================================================
# BRIEF RECAP
# ============================================================

Last time, a letter arrived from Raskolnikov's mother.

His sister Dounia is engaged to marry Pyotr Petrovitch Luzhin—a "practical man" of forty-five who wants a wife who will look upon him as her benefactor.

Raskolnikov saw through it immediately.

Dounia is selling herself.

Just as Sonia sold herself.

For him.

[pause-2]

And somewhere in his mind, the idea—the thing—began to crystallize into necessity.

"I shall go to Razumihin... on the next day after It."

[pause-2]

Now, exhausted, feverish, he has collapsed in the bushes on Petrovsky Island.

And he begins to dream.

# ============================================================
# PART ONE: THE STORY
# Chapter 5 - The Dream
# ============================================================

[pause-3]

In a morbid condition of the brain, dreams often have a singular actuality.

A vividness.

An extraordinary semblance of reality.

[pause-2]

Monstrous images may be created.

But the setting, the details, are so truthlike, so artistically consistent—

That the dreamer, were he an artist like Pushkin or Turgenev, could never have invented them in waking life.

[pause-2]

Such sick dreams remain long in the memory.

They make a powerful impression on the overwrought nervous system.

[pause-3]

Raskolnikov has such a dream now.

[pause-5]

He dreams he is back in his childhood.

In the little town of his birth.

He is a child, about seven years old.

Walking into the country with his father on the evening of a holiday.

[pause-2]

It is a grey and heavy day.

The country is exactly as he remembers it—indeed, more vividly than in waking memory.

The little town on a level flat, bare as a hand.

Not even a willow nearby.

Only far in the distance, a dark copse on the horizon.

[pause-3]

A few paces beyond the last market garden stands a tavern.

A big tavern.

It has always aroused in him a feeling of aversion.

Even of fear.

[pause-2]

There is always a crowd there.

Shouting, laughter, abuse.

Hideous hoarse singing.

Often fighting.

Drunken, horrible-looking figures hanging about.

[pause-2]

He used to cling close to his father, trembling, when they passed.

[pause-3]

Near the tavern, the road becomes a dusty track.

The dust is always black.

About a hundred paces further, it turns right to the graveyard.

[pause-2]

In the middle of the graveyard stands a stone church with a green cupola.

He used to go there two or three times a year with his father and mother—

When a service was held in memory of his grandmother, long dead.

[pause-2]

On these occasions, they took a special sort of rice pudding with raisins stuck in the shape of a cross.

He loved that church.

The old-fashioned icons.

The old priest with his shaking head.

[pause-2]

Near his grandmother's grave, marked by a stone, was the little grave of his younger brother.

Dead at six months old.

He did not remember him.

But he had been told about his little brother.

[pause-2]

And whenever he visited the graveyard, he would reverently cross himself and bow down and kiss the little grave.

[pause-5]

Now, in the dream, he is walking with his father past the tavern on the way to the graveyard.

He holds his father's hand.

He looks with dread at the tavern.

[pause-2]

A peculiar circumstance attracts his attention.

There seems to be some kind of festivity.

Crowds of gaily dressed townspeople.

Peasant women.

Their husbands.

Riffraff of all sorts.

All singing.

All more or less drunk.

[pause-3]

Near the entrance of the tavern stands a cart.

A strange cart.

[pause-2]

It is one of those big carts usually drawn by heavy cart-horses.

Loaded with casks of wine or other heavy goods.

He has always liked watching those great cart-horses.

Their long manes.

Their thick legs.

Their slow, even pace.

Drawing along a perfect mountain with no appearance of effort.

[pause-3]

But now—strange to say—in the shafts of such a cart he sees a thin little sorrel beast.

A peasant's nag.

[pause-2]

One of those wretched creatures he has seen straining under heavy loads of wood or hay.

Especially when the wheels are stuck in the mud.

[pause-2]

And the peasants beat them so cruelly.

Sometimes even about the nose and eyes.

He always felt so sorry.

So sorry for them that he almost cried.

And his mother would take him away from the window.

[pause-5]

Now there is a great uproar.

Shouting, singing, the balalaika.

From the tavern, a number of big, very drunken peasants come out.

Wearing red and blue shirts.

Coats thrown over their shoulders.

[pause-2]

"Get in, get in!" shouts one of them.

A young, thick-necked peasant with a fleshy face red as a carrot.

"I'll take you all, get in!"

[pause-2]

Laughter and exclamations burst from the crowd.

"Take us all with a beast like that!"

"Why, Mikolka, are you crazy to put a nag like that in such a cart?"

"And this mare is twenty if she is a day!"

[pause-3]

"Get in, I'll take you all!" Mikolka shouts again.

He leaps into the cart, seizes the reins, stands straight up in front.

"The bay has gone with Matvey, and this brute is just breaking my heart."

"I feel as if I could kill her."

"She's just eating her head off."

"Get in, I tell you! I'll make her gallop! She'll gallop!"

[pause-2]

He picks up the whip, preparing himself with relish.

[pause-3]

"Get in! Come along!" the crowd laughs.

"D'you hear, she'll gallop!"

"Gallop indeed! She has not had a gallop in her for the last ten years!"

"She'll jog along!"

"Don't you mind her, mates, bring a whip each of you, get ready!"

"All right! Give it to her!"

[pause-2]

They all clamber in, laughing and making jokes.

Six men get in, and there is still room for more.

They haul in a fat, rosy-cheeked woman.

She is cracking nuts and laughing.

[pause-3]

The crowd laughs too.

How could they help it?

That wretched nag is to drag all of them at a gallop!

[pause-2]

Two young fellows get whips ready to help Mikolka.

[pause-2]

With the cry of "Now!" the mare tugs with all her might.

But far from galloping, she can scarcely move forward.

She struggles with her legs.

Gasping.

Shrinking from the blows that shower upon her like hail.

[pause-3]

The laughter redoubles.

But Mikolka flies into a rage.

He thrashes the mare furiously, as though he really believes she can gallop.

[pause-2]

"Let me get in too, mates!" shouts a young man whose appetite is aroused.

"Get in, all get in," cries Mikolka.

"She will draw you all! I'll beat her to death!"

[pause-2]

And he thrashes and thrashes at the mare.

Beside himself with fury.

[pause-5]

"Father, father," the boy cries.

"Father, what are they doing? Father, they are beating the poor horse!"

[pause-2]

"Come along, come along!" his father says.

"They are drunk, they are foolish, it's none of our business. Come away, don't look!"

[pause-2]

He tries to draw him away.

But the boy tears himself from his hand.

Beside himself with horror, he runs to the horse.

[pause-3]

The poor beast is in a bad way.

Gasping.

Standing still.

Then tugging again.

Almost falling.

[pause-2]

"Beat her to death!" cries Mikolka.

"It's come to that. I'll do for her!"

[pause-2]

"What are you about, are you a Christian, you devil?" shouts an old man in the crowd.

"Did anyone ever see the like? A wretched nag like that pulling such a cartload!" says another.

"You'll kill her!" shouts a third.

[pause-2]

"Don't meddle! It's my property! I'll do what I choose!"

"Get in, more of you! Get in, all of you!"

"I will have her go at a gallop!"

[pause-3]

All at once, laughter breaks into a roar.

The mare, roused by the blows, has begun feebly kicking.

Even the old man cannot help smiling.

A wretched little beast like that—trying to kick!

[pause-2]

Two lads in the crowd snatch up whips and run to beat her about the ribs.

One on each side.

[pause-2]

"Hit her in the face, in the eyes, in the eyes!" cries Mikolka.

[pause-2]

"Give us a song, mates!" someone shouts.

And they join in a riotous song.

Jingling a tambourine.

Whistling.

The woman goes on cracking nuts and laughing.

[pause-5]

The boy runs beside the mare.

Runs in front of her.

Sees her being whipped across the eyes.

Right in the eyes!

[pause-2]

He is crying.

He feels choking.

His tears are streaming.

[pause-2]

One of the men gives him a cut with the whip across the face.

He does not feel it.

[pause-2]

Wringing his hands and screaming, he rushes up to a grey-bearded old man who is shaking his head in disapproval.

A woman seizes the boy and tries to take him away.

But he tears himself free and runs back to the mare.

[pause-3]

She is almost at the last gasp.

But she begins kicking once more.

[pause-2]

"I'll teach you to kick!" Mikolka shouts ferociously.

[pause-2]

He throws down the whip.

Bends forward.

Picks up from the bottom of the cart a long, thick shaft.

Takes hold of one end with both hands.

Brandishes it over the mare.

[pause-3]

"He'll crush her!" someone shouts.

"He'll kill her!"

[pause-2]

"It's my property!" Mikolka screams.

[pause-2]

And brings the shaft down with a swinging blow.

[pause-2]

There is a sound of a heavy thud.

[pause-3]

"Thrash her, thrash her! Why have you stopped?"

[pause-2]

Mikolka swings a second time.

It falls on the spine of the luckless mare.

She sinks back on her haunches.

But lurches forward.

Tugs forward with all her force.

First on one side, then on the other.

Trying to move the cart.

[pause-2]

But six whips are attacking her from all directions.

And the shaft is raised again.

And falls a third time.

Then a fourth.

Heavy, measured blows.

[pause-2]

Mikolka is in a fury that he cannot kill her at one blow.

[pause-3]

"She's a tough one!" someone shouts.

"She'll fall in a minute, mates. There will soon be an end of her."

"Fetch an axe to her! Finish her off!"

[pause-2]

"I'll show you! Stand off!"

[pause-2]

Mikolka throws down the shaft.

Stoops down in the cart.

Picks up an iron crowbar.

"Look out," he shouts.

[pause-2]

And with all his might, he deals a stunning blow at the poor mare.

[pause-3]

The blow falls.

The mare staggers.

Sinks back.

Tries to pull.

But the crowbar falls again on her back.

[pause-2]

And she falls on the ground like a log.

[pause-3]

"Finish her off!" Mikolka shouts.

He leaps out of the cart, beside himself.

[pause-2]

Several young men, also flushed with drink, seize whatever they can find.

Whips.

Sticks.

Poles.

[pause-2]

They run to the dying mare.

[pause-2]

Mikolka stands on one side.

Dealing random blows with the crowbar.

[pause-2]

The mare stretches out her head.

Draws a long breath.

And dies.

[pause-3]

"You butchered her!" someone shouts in the crowd.

"Why wouldn't she gallop!"

"My property!" shouts Mikolka, with bloodshot eyes, brandishing the crowbar.

[pause-2]

He stands as though regretting that he has nothing more to beat.

[pause-2]

"No mistake about it, you are not a Christian!" many voices shout.

[pause-5]

But the poor boy is beside himself.

[pause-2]

He pushes through the crowd to the sorrel nag.

Puts his arms around her bleeding dead head.

And kisses it.

Kisses the eyes.

Kisses the lips.

[pause-3]

Then he jumps up.

Flies at Mikolka in a frenzy with his little fists.

[pause-2]

At that instant, his father snatches him up and carries him out of the crowd.

[pause-2]

"Come along, come! Let us go home," he says.

[pause-2]

"Father! Why did they... kill... the poor horse!" the boy sobs.

But his voice breaks.

The words come in shrieks from his panting chest.

[pause-2]

"They are drunk. They are brutal. It's not our business!"

[pause-2]

The boy puts his arms around his father.

But he feels choked.

Choked.

He tries to draw a breath.

To cry out—

[pause-5]

And wakes up.

[pause-5]

He wakes gasping for breath.

His hair soaked with perspiration.

He stands up in terror.

[pause-3]

"Thank God, that was only a dream," he says, sitting down under a tree and drawing deep breaths.

"But what is it? Is it some fever coming on? Such a hideous dream!"

[pause-3]

He feels utterly broken.

Darkness and confusion are in his soul.

He rests his elbows on his knees.

Leans his head on his hands.

[pause-3]

"Good God!" he cries.

"Can it be, can it be, that I shall really take an axe, that I shall strike her on the head, split her skull open..."

"That I shall tread in the sticky warm blood, break the lock, steal and tremble; hide, all spattered in blood... with the axe..."

"Good God, can it be?"

[pause-3]

He is shaking like a leaf.

[pause-3]

"But why am I going on like this?" he continues, sitting up as if in profound amazement.

"I knew that I could never bring myself to it."

"So what have I been torturing myself for till now?"

[pause-2]

"Yesterday, yesterday, when I went to make that... experiment..."

"Yesterday I realized completely that I could never bear to do it."

[pause-2]

"Why am I going over it again, then?"

"Why am I hesitating?"

[pause-2]

"As I came down the stairs yesterday, I said myself that it was base, loathsome, vile, vile..."

"The very thought of it made me feel sick and filled me with horror."

[pause-3]

"No, I couldn't do it, I couldn't do it!"

[pause-2]

"Granted, granted that there is no flaw in all that reasoning."

"That all I have concluded this last month is clear as day, true as arithmetic."

"My God!"

"Anyway I couldn't bring myself to it!"

"I couldn't do it, I couldn't do it!"

[pause-2]

"Why, why then am I still..."

[pause-5]

He rises to his feet.

Looks around in wonder, as though surprised to find himself in this place.

And walks toward the bridge.

[pause-2]

He is pale.

His eyes glow.

He is exhausted in every limb.

[pause-2]

But he seems suddenly to breathe more easily.

He feels he has cast off a fearful burden.

All at once there is a sense of relief and peace in his soul.

[pause-3]

"Lord," he prays, "show me my path. I renounce that accursed... dream of mine."

[pause-3]

Crossing the bridge, he gazes quietly and calmly at the Neva.

At the glowing red sun setting in the glowing sky.

[pause-2]

In spite of his weakness, he is not conscious of fatigue.

It is as though an abscess that had been forming for a month in his heart had suddenly broken.

[pause-2]

Freedom, freedom!

He is free from that spell, that sorcery, that obsession!

[pause-5]

# ============================================================
# Chapter 6 - The Coincidence
# ============================================================

Later, when he recalls this time, he is superstitiously impressed by one circumstance.

Though in itself not very exceptional, it always seems to him the predestined turning point of his fate.

[pause-2]

He can never understand why, when he was tired and worn out—

When it would have been more convenient to go home by the shortest way—

He returned instead by way of the Hay Market.

Where he had no need to go.

[pause-2]

It was obviously out of his way.

Though not by much.

[pause-2]

But why, he would always ask himself later—

Why had such an important, such a decisive, and at the same time such an absolutely chance meeting happened in the Hay Market?

At the very hour, the very minute of his life—

When he was just in the mood and circumstances in which that meeting could exert the gravest influence on his whole destiny?

[pause-2]

As though it had been lying in wait for him on purpose.

[pause-5]

It is about nine o'clock when he crosses the Hay Market.

[pause-2]

At the tables and barrows, at the booths and shops, the market people are closing up.

Clearing away their wares.

Going home.

[pause-2]

Rag pickers and costermongers crowd around the taverns in the dirty, stinking courtyards.

Raskolnikov has always liked this place.

Here his rags do not attract contemptuous attention.

One can walk about in any attire without scandalizing people.

[pause-3]

At the corner of an alley, a huckster and his wife have two tables set out.

Tapes, thread, cotton handkerchiefs.

They too are getting ready to go home.

But they are lingering in conversation with a friend who has just come up to them.

[pause-2]

This friend is Lizaveta Ivanovna.

[pause-2]

Or, as everyone calls her, Lizaveta.

The younger sister of the old pawnbroker.

Alyona Ivanovna.

Whom Raskolnikov had visited the previous day to pawn his watch and make his experiment.

[pause-3]

He already knows all about Lizaveta.

And she knows him a little too.

[pause-2]

She is a single woman of about thirty-five.

Tall.

Clumsy.

Timid.

Submissive.

Almost idiotic.

[pause-2]

She is a complete slave to her sister.

Works for her day and night.

Goes in fear and trembling of her.

Even suffers beatings from her.

[pause-3]

She stands now with a bundle before the huckster and his wife.

Listening earnestly and doubtfully.

They are talking of something with special warmth.

[pause-2]

The moment Raskolnikov catches sight of her, he is overcome by a strange sensation.

A kind of intense astonishment.

Though there is nothing astonishing about this meeting.

[pause-3]

"You could make up your mind for yourself, Lizaveta Ivanovna," the huckster is saying aloud.

"Come round tomorrow about seven. They will be here too."

[pause-2]

"Tomorrow?" says Lizaveta slowly and thoughtfully.

As though unable to make up her mind.

[pause-2]

"Upon my word, what a fright you are in of Alyona Ivanovna!" gabbles the huckster's wife.

"I look at you, you are like some little babe."

"And she is not your own sister either—nothing but a stepsister."

"And what a hand she keeps over you!"

[pause-2]

"But this time don't say a word to Alyona Ivanovna," the husband interrupts.

"That's my advice. Come round to us without asking. It will be worth your while."

"Later on, your sister herself may have a notion."

[pause-2]

"Am I to come?"

[pause-2]

"About seven o'clock tomorrow. And they will be here. You will be able to decide for yourself."

[pause-2]

"And we'll have a cup of tea," the wife adds.

[pause-2]

"All right, I'll come," says Lizaveta, still pondering.

And she begins slowly moving away.

[pause-5]

Raskolnikov has just passed.

He heard no more.

[pause-2]

He passed softly.

Unnoticed.

Trying not to miss a word.

[pause-3]

His first amazement is followed by a thrill of horror.

Like a shiver running down his spine.

[pause-2]

He has learned—suddenly, quite unexpectedly—

That the next day at seven o'clock, Lizaveta, the old woman's sister and only companion—

Will be away from home.

[pause-2]

And therefore at seven o'clock precisely—

The old woman will be left alone.

[pause-5]

He is only a few steps from his lodging.

He goes in like a man condemned to death.

[pause-2]

He thinks of nothing.

He is incapable of thinking.

[pause-2]

But he feels suddenly in his whole being—

That he has no more freedom of thought.

No will.

[pause-2]

And that everything is suddenly and irrevocably decided.

[pause-5]

Certainly, if he had to wait whole years for a suitable opportunity—

He could not reckon on a more certain step toward success than what has just presented itself.

[pause-2]

In any case, it would have been difficult to find out beforehand—

With such certainty, with greater exactness, and with less risk—

Without dangerous inquiries and investigations—

That the next day at a certain time, an old woman on whose life an attempt was contemplated—

Would be at home and entirely alone.

[pause-5]

# ============================================================
# The Background of the Theory
# ============================================================

Later, Raskolnikov finds out why the huckster and his wife had invited Lizaveta.

It was a very ordinary matter.

A family had come to town and needed to sell their household goods.

Lizaveta undertook such jobs frequently.

She was honest and always fixed a fair price.

[pause-3]

But Raskolnikov has become superstitious of late.

The traces of superstition remain in him long after.

Almost ineradicable.

[pause-2]

In all this, he is always afterward disposed to see something strange and mysterious.

As if there were some peculiar influences and coincidences at work.

[pause-3]

The previous winter, a student named Pokorev had given him the pawnbroker's address.

In case he ever needed to pawn anything.

[pause-2]

For a long while, he did not go to her.

He had lessons.

He managed somehow.

[pause-2]

Six weeks ago, he remembered the address.

He had two things to pawn: his father's silver watch and a gold ring from his sister.

He decided to take the ring.

[pause-2]

When he found the old woman, he felt an insurmountable repulsion at the first glance.

Though he knew nothing special about her.

He got two roubles from her and went into a miserable little tavern on his way home.

[pause-2]

He asked for tea, sat down, and sank into deep thought.

[pause-2]

A strange idea was pecking at his brain like a chicken in the egg.

And very, very much absorbed him.

[pause-5]

Almost beside him at the next table sat a student he did not know.

And with him, a young officer.

They had played billiards and were drinking tea.

[pause-2]

All at once, Raskolnikov heard the student mention the pawnbroker Alyona Ivanovna.

And give her address to the officer.

[pause-2]

This seemed strange.

He had just come from her, and here he heard her name.

Of course it was a chance.

But he could not shake off a very extraordinary impression.

[pause-2]

Here, someone seemed to be speaking expressly for him.

[pause-3]

The student began telling his friend details about Alyona Ivanovna.

[pause-2]

"She is first-rate. You can always get money from her."

"She is rich as a Jew, can give you five thousand roubles at a time."

"And she is not above taking a pledge for a rouble."

"Lots of our fellows have had dealings with her."

"But she is an awful old harpy..."

[pause-2]

He described how spiteful and uncertain she was.

How if you were only a day late with your interest, the pledge was lost.

How she gave a quarter of the value and took five or seven percent a month.

[pause-3]

The student chattered on.

He said she had a sister, Lizaveta.

Whom the wretched old creature was continually beating.

And kept in complete bondage like a small child.

Though Lizaveta was at least six feet high.

[pause-2]

"There's a phenomenon for you," the student cried, laughing.

[pause-3]

They began talking about Lizaveta.

The student spoke of her with peculiar relish.

She was not hideous, he said, despite her height and clumsiness.

She had a good-natured face and kind eyes.

Lots of people were attracted to her.

[pause-2]

"She is such a soft, gentle creature. Ready to put up with anything. Always willing."

"And her smile is really very sweet."

[pause-2]

"You seem to find her attractive yourself," laughed the officer.

"From her queerness."

[pause-3]

Then—the student's voice changed.

[pause-2]

"No, I'll tell you what."

"I could kill that damned old woman and make off with her money."

"I assure you, without the faintest conscience-prick."

[pause-3]

The officer laughed again.

But Raskolnikov shuddered.

[pause-2]

How strange it was!

[pause-3]

"Listen, I want to ask you a serious question," the student said hotly.

"I was joking, of course. But look here."

[pause-2]

"On one side, we have a stupid, senseless, worthless, spiteful, ailing, horrid old woman."

"Not simply useless, but doing actual mischief."

"Who has not an idea what she is living for herself."

"And who will die in a day or two in any case."

"You understand?"

[pause-2]

"Yes, yes, I understand."

[pause-2]

"Well, listen then."

"On the other side, fresh young lives thrown away for want of help."

"By thousands, on every side!"

[pause-2]

"A hundred thousand good deeds could be done and helped with that old woman's money."

"Which will otherwise be buried in a monastery!"

[pause-2]

"Hundreds, thousands perhaps, might be set on the right path."

"Dozens of families saved from destitution, from ruin, from vice, from the hospitals."

"And all with her money."

[pause-3]

"Kill her, take her money, and with the help of it—"

"Devote oneself to the service of humanity and the good of all."

[pause-2]

"What do you think?"

"Would not one tiny crime be wiped out by thousands of good deeds?"

"For one life, thousands would be saved from corruption and decay."

"One death, and a hundred lives in exchange—it's simple arithmetic!"

[pause-3]

"Besides, what value has the life of that sickly, stupid, ill-natured old woman in the balance of existence!"

"No more than the life of a louse."

"Of a black beetle."

"Less, in fact, because the old woman is doing harm."

"She is wearing out the lives of others."

"The other day she bit Lizaveta's finger out of spite. It almost had to be amputated."

[pause-3]

"Of course she does not deserve to live," the officer remarked.

"But there it is. It's nature."

[pause-2]

"Oh, well, brother, but we have to correct and direct nature."

"And but for that, we should drown in an ocean of prejudice."

"But for that, there would never have been a single great man."

[pause-2]

"They talk of duty, conscience—I don't want to say anything against duty and conscience."

"But the point is: what do we mean by them?"

[pause-3]

"Stay, I have another question for you. Listen."

"You are talking and speechifying away."

"But tell me—would you kill the old woman yourself?"

[pause-3]

"Of course not! I was only arguing the justice of it."

"It's nothing to do with me."

[pause-2]

"But I think, if you would not do it yourself, there's no justice about it."

"Let us have another game."

[pause-5]

Raskolnikov was violently agitated.

[pause-2]

Of course, it was all ordinary youthful talk and thought.

Such as he had often heard before, in different forms and on different themes.

[pause-2]

But why had he happened to hear such a discussion at the very moment—

When his own brain was conceiving the very same ideas?

[pause-2]

And why, just at the moment when he had brought away the embryo of his idea from the old woman—

Had he dropped at once upon a conversation about her?

[pause-3]

This coincidence always seemed strange to him.

[pause-2]

This trivial talk in a tavern had an immense influence on him in his later action.

As though there had really been something preordained in it.

Some guiding hint.

[pause-5]

# DISCUSSION (Less than 5 minutes)

So.

Let us pause here.

[pause-3]

The dream.

What do we make of it?

[pause-2]

Dostoevsky understood something that psychoanalysis would not articulate for another forty years.

That dreams speak what the waking mind cannot.

[pause-3]

Raskolnikov has been telling himself that his "idea" is rational.

A matter of arithmetic.

One death for a hundred lives.

Simple calculation.

[pause-2]

But the dream shows the truth.

[pause-2]

He is the boy.

Running toward the beaten horse.

Kissing its bloody face.

Flying at the murderer with his tiny fists.

[pause-3]

"It's my property!" Mikolka screams.

[pause-2]

That is the logic Raskolnikov has been entertaining.

The old woman is a louse.

A beetle.

A thing to be disposed of.

[pause-2]

But the boy in him knows better.

The boy cannot watch a creature suffer without trying to save it.

The boy weeps.

[pause-3]

Notice what happens when Raskolnikov wakes.

[pause-2]

He renounces the idea.

He feels free.

He prays.

He walks toward the sunset with peace in his heart.

[pause-2]

The dream has done its work.

The conscience has spoken.

[pause-3]

And then—the coincidence.

[pause-2]

Walking home, taking a route he has no reason to take—

He overhears that Lizaveta will be away tomorrow at seven.

The old woman will be alone.

[pause-3]

"He felt suddenly in his whole being that he had no more freedom of thought, no will."

"And that everything was suddenly and irrevocably decided."

[pause-2]

What are we to make of this?

[pause-3]

Is it fate?

Is it the devil?

Is it simply chance—and Raskolnikov's interpretation of chance?

[pause-2]

Dostoevsky leaves the question open.

[pause-2]

But notice the pattern.

[pause-2]

Whenever Raskolnikov tries to turn away from the idea—

Something pulls him back.

The letter from his mother.

The conversation in the tavern about the old woman.

Now this overheard detail about Lizaveta.

[pause-3]

He keeps finding "reasons."

Justifications.

Signs.

[pause-2]

And each time, he surrenders his will a little more.

"Everything is suddenly and irrevocably decided."

[pause-2]

But is it?

Or is he simply refusing to decide?

Letting circumstance choose for him?

[pause-3]

This is the seduction of the theory.

[pause-2]

If you can convince yourself that you have no choice—

Then you are not responsible.

Then it is not really you who acts.

Then you are merely an instrument of fate.

Or history.

Or some higher purpose.

[pause-3]

The great criminals of history have always reasoned this way.

[pause-2]

"I had no choice."

"It was necessary."

"The times demanded it."

[pause-3]

Dostoevsky is showing us how a man talks himself into the unthinkable.

[pause-2]

First, the theory: that extraordinary men have the right to transgress.

Then, the circumstances: poverty, family sacrifice, wounded pride.

Then, the "signs": coincidences that seem like permission.

[pause-2]

And finally—the surrender.

"Everything is decided."

[pause-3]

Next time, we will follow Raskolnikov through the final preparations.

And into the old woman's apartment.

[pause-2]

The axe will fall.

And nothing will ever be the same.

[pause-3]

Until then.
