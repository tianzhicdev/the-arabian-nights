# Crime and Punishment: Episode 4 - "The Murder"
# Narrated by Celeste
# Runtime: Approximately 30-35 minutes

# ============================================================
# HIGHLIGHT (30 seconds - 1 minute)
# ============================================================

The door opens a tiny crack.

Two sharp, suspicious eyes stare at him out of the darkness.

[pause-2]

"Good evening, Alyona Ivanovna," he begins.

His voice will not obey him.

It breaks and shakes.

"I have come... I have brought something... but we'd better come in... to the light."

[pause-5]

# ============================================================
# INTRODUCTION
# ============================================================

This is Celeste, telling you the story from Crime and Punishment.

[pause-3]

# ============================================================
# BRIEF RECAP
# ============================================================

Last time, Raskolnikov dreamed of a mare beaten to death by drunken peasants.

"It's my property!" the killer screamed.

And a boy—Raskolnikov as a child—ran to kiss the horse's bloody face.

[pause-2]

He woke in terror.

Renounced the idea.

Felt free.

[pause-2]

But walking home through the Hay Market, he overheard a conversation.

Lizaveta, the pawnbroker's sister, will be away tomorrow at seven.

The old woman will be alone.

[pause-2]

"Everything is suddenly and irrevocably decided."

[pause-3]

Today, we reach the center of the novel.

The act that cannot be undone.

# ============================================================
# PART ONE: THE STORY
# Chapter 6 (continued) - The Final Day
# ============================================================

[pause-3]

On returning from the Hay Market, Raskolnikov flings himself on the sofa.

He sits for a whole hour without stirring.

Meanwhile, it grows dark.

He has no candle.

Indeed, it does not occur to him to light one.

[pause-2]

He could never recollect afterward whether he had been thinking about anything during that time.

[pause-2]

At last, he is conscious of his former fever and shivering.

He realizes with relief that he can lie down on the sofa.

[pause-2]

Soon, heavy, leaden sleep comes over him.

Crushing him.

[pause-3]

He sleeps an extraordinarily long time.

Without dreaming.

[pause-2]

Nastasya, coming into his room at ten o'clock the next morning, has difficulty rousing him.

She brings tea and bread.

The tea is again her own—the second brewing from her own teapot.

[pause-2]

"My goodness, how he sleeps!"

[pause-2]

He gets up with an effort.

His head aches.

He stands up, takes a turn in his garret, and sinks back on the sofa again.

[pause-2]

"Going to sleep again? Are you ill?"

He makes no reply.

[pause-2]

"Do you want some tea?"

"Afterwards," he says with an effort, closing his eyes.

[pause-3]

She comes in again at two o'clock with soup.

He is lying as before.

The tea stands untouched.

[pause-2]

She feels offended and begins rousing him.

"Why are you lying like a log?"

[pause-2]

He sits up but says nothing.

Stares at the floor.

[pause-2]

"Are you ill or not?"

No answer.

[pause-2]

"You'd better go out and get a breath of air. Will you eat it or not?"

"Afterwards," he says weakly. "You can go."

[pause-2]

She remains a little longer, looks at him with compassion, and goes out.

[pause-3]

A few minutes afterward, he raises his eyes.

Looks for a long while at the tea and the soup.

Then he takes the bread, picks up a spoon, and begins to eat.

[pause-2]

He eats a little.

Three or four spoonfuls.

Without appetite.

Mechanically.

[pause-2]

His head aches less.

[pause-2]

After his meal, he stretches himself on the sofa again.

But now he cannot sleep.

He lies without stirring, with his face in the pillow.

[pause-3]

He is haunted by daydreams.

Such strange daydreams.

[pause-2]

In one that keeps recurring, he fancies he is in Africa.

In Egypt.

In some sort of oasis.

[pause-2]

The caravan is resting.

The camels are peacefully lying down.

The palms stand all around in a complete circle.

All the party are at dinner.

[pause-2]

But he is drinking water from a spring which flows gurgling close by.

It is so cool.

Wonderful.

Blue, cold water running among the colored stones and over the clean sand.

[pause-5]

Suddenly, he hears a clock strike.

[pause-2]

He starts.

Rouses himself.

Raises his head.

Looks out of the window.

[pause-2]

Seeing how late it is, he suddenly jumps up wide awake—

As though someone had pulled him off the sofa.

[pause-3]

He creeps on tiptoe to the door.

Stealthily opens it.

Begins listening on the staircase.

His heart beats terribly.

[pause-2]

But all is quiet on the stairs.

As if everyone were asleep.

[pause-2]

It seems strange and monstrous to him—

That he could have slept in such forgetfulness from the previous day.

And had done nothing.

Had prepared nothing yet.

[pause-2]

And meanwhile, perhaps it has already struck six.

[pause-3]

His drowsiness gives way to an extraordinary, feverish haste.

But the preparations to be made are few.

[pause-2]

He concentrates all his energies on thinking of everything and forgetting nothing.

And his heart keeps beating so that he can hardly breathe.

[pause-3]

First, he has to make a noose and sew it into his overcoat.

A work of a moment.

[pause-2]

He rummages under his pillow.

Picks out a worn, old shirt.

Tears from its rags a long strip, about two inches wide.

[pause-2]

He folds this strip in two.

Takes off his wide summer overcoat—his only outer garment.

And begins sewing the two ends of the rag on the inside, under the left armhole.

[pause-2]

His hands shake as he sews.

But he does it successfully.

Nothing shows outside when he puts the coat on again.

[pause-3]

The noose is intended for the axe.

[pause-2]

It is impossible to carry an axe through the street in his hands.

And if hidden under his coat, he would still have to support it with his hand—

Which would be noticeable.

[pause-2]

Now, he has only to put the head of the axe in the noose.

It will hang quietly under his arm on the inside.

Putting his hand in his coat pocket, he can hold the end of the handle all the way.

[pause-2]

This noose he had designed a fortnight before.

[pause-3]

Next, he draws out the pledge he had prepared long before.

[pause-2]

This pledge is only a smoothly planed piece of wood.

The size and thickness of a silver cigarette case.

[pause-2]

He had wrapped it carefully in clean white paper.

And tied up the parcel so that it would be very difficult to untie.

[pause-2]

This was to divert the attention of the old woman for a time.

While she was trying to undo the knot, he would gain a moment.

[pause-3]

He had only just got the pledge out when he heard someone shout in the yard.

"It struck six long ago."

"Long ago! My God!"

[pause-2]

He rushes to the door.

Listens.

Catches up his hat.

And begins to descend his thirteen steps cautiously, noiselessly, like a cat.

[pause-3]

He has still the most important thing to do—to steal the axe from the kitchen.

[pause-2]

That the deed must be done with an axe, he had decided long ago.

He had also a pocket pruning-knife.

But he could not rely on the knife, and still less on his own strength.

So he resolved finally on the axe.

[pause-5]

# ============================================================
# The Axe
# ============================================================

One trifling circumstance upsets his calculations before he even leaves the staircase.

[pause-2]

When he reaches the landlady's kitchen—the door of which is open as usual—

He glances cautiously in to see whether Nastasya is there.

[pause-3]

But what is his amazement—

When he suddenly sees that Nastasya is not only at home in the kitchen—

But is occupied there, taking linen out of a basket and hanging it on a line.

[pause-2]

Seeing him, she leaves off hanging the clothes.

Turns to him.

And stares at him all the time he is passing.

[pause-2]

He turns away his eyes.

Walks past as though he noticed nothing.

[pause-2]

But it is the end of everything.

He has not the axe!

He is overwhelmed.

[pause-3]

"What made me think," he reflects, as he goes under the gateway—

"What made me think that she would be sure not to be at home at that moment?"

"Why, why, why did I assume this so certainly?"

[pause-2]

He is crushed and even humiliated.

He could have laughed at himself in his anger.

A dull, animal rage boils within him.

[pause-3]

He stands hesitating in the gateway.

To go into the street, to go for a walk for appearance's sake—revolting.

To go back to his room—even more revolting.

[pause-2]

"And what a chance I have lost forever!" he mutters.

Standing aimlessly in the gateway.

Just opposite the porter's little dark room, which is also open.

[pause-3]

Suddenly, he starts.

[pause-2]

From the porter's room, two paces away from him—

Something shining under the bench to the right catches his eye.

[pause-2]

He looks about him.

Nobody.

[pause-2]

He approaches the room on tiptoe.

Goes down two steps into it.

And in a faint voice calls the porter.

[pause-2]

"Yes, not at home! Somewhere near though, in the yard, for the door is wide open."

[pause-2]

He dashes to the axe—it is an axe—

And pulls it out from under the bench, where it lay between two chunks of wood.

[pause-2]

At once, before going out, he makes it fast in the noose.

Thrusts both hands into his pockets.

And goes out of the room.

[pause-2]

No one had noticed him.

[pause-3]

"When reason fails, the devil helps!" he thinks with a strange grin.

[pause-2]

This chance raises his spirits extraordinarily.

[pause-5]

# ============================================================
# The Walk
# ============================================================

He walks along quietly and sedately, without hurry.

To avoid awakening suspicion.

[pause-2]

He scarcely looks at the passersby.

Tries to escape looking at their faces at all.

To be as little noticeable as possible.

[pause-2]

Suddenly, he thinks of his hat.

"Good heavens! I had the money the day before yesterday and did not get a cap to wear instead!"

[pause-2]

A curse rises from the bottom of his soul.

[pause-3]

Glancing out of the corner of his eye into a shop, he sees by a clock on the wall—

It is ten minutes past seven.

[pause-2]

He has to make haste.

And at the same time go some way round.

To approach the house from the other side.

[pause-3]

When he had imagined all this beforehand, he had sometimes thought he would be very much afraid.

But he is not very much afraid now.

Not afraid at all, indeed.

[pause-2]

His mind is even occupied by irrelevant matters.

But by nothing for long.

[pause-2]

As he passes the Yusupov garden, he is deeply absorbed in considering the building of great fountains.

And their refreshing effect on the atmosphere in all the squares.

[pause-3]

"So, probably, men led to execution clutch mentally at every object that meets them on the way."

[pause-2]

This flashes through his mind.

But simply flashes, like lightning.

He makes haste to dismiss the thought.

[pause-3]

And by now he is near.

Here is the house.

Here is the gate.

[pause-2]

Suddenly, a clock somewhere strikes once.

"What! Can it be half-past seven? Impossible, it must be fast!"

[pause-3]

Luckily, everything goes well again at the gates.

[pause-2]

At that very moment, as though expressly for his benefit—

A huge wagon of hay has just driven in at the gate.

Completely screening him as he passes under the gateway.

[pause-2]

The wagon has scarcely had time to drive through into the yard—

Before he slips in a flash to the right.

[pause-2]

On the other side of the wagon, he can hear shouting and quarreling.

But no one notices him.

No one meets him.

[pause-3]

The staircase leading to the old woman's room is close by.

Just on the right of the gateway.

He is already on the stairs.

[pause-3]

Drawing a breath.

Pressing his hand against his throbbing heart.

Once more feeling for the axe and setting it straight.

He begins softly and cautiously ascending the stairs.

Listening every minute.

[pause-2]

But the stairs too are quite deserted.

All the doors are shut.

He meets no one.

[pause-2]

One flat on the first floor is wide open, and painters are at work in it.

But they do not glance at him.

[pause-2]

He stands still, thinks a minute, and goes on.

"Of course, it would be better if they had not been here, but... it's two stories above them."

[pause-3]

And here is the fourth floor.

Here is the door.

Here is the flat opposite—the empty one.

[pause-2]

He is out of breath.

For one instant, the thought floats through his mind: "Shall I go back?"

[pause-2]

But he makes no answer.

And begins listening at the old woman's door.

A dead silence.

[pause-2]

Then he listens again on the staircase.

Listens long and intently.

[pause-2]

Then looks about him for the last time.

Pulls himself together.

Draws himself up.

And once more tries the axe in the noose.

[pause-2]

"Am I very pale?" he wonders.

"Am I not evidently agitated?"

"She is mistrustful... Had I better wait a little longer... till my heart leaves off thumping?"

[pause-3]

But his heart does not leave off.

On the contrary, as though to spite him—

It throbs more and more violently.

[pause-2]

He can stand it no longer.

[pause-2]

He slowly puts out his hand to the bell.

And rings.

[pause-3]

Half a minute later, he rings again.

More loudly.

[pause-2]

No answer.

[pause-2]

To go on ringing is useless and out of place.

The old woman is, of course, at home.

But she is suspicious and alone.

[pause-2]

He has some knowledge of her habits.

And once more, he puts his ear to the door.

[pause-2]

Either his senses are peculiarly keen—

Or the sound is really very distinct.

[pause-2]

He suddenly hears something like the cautious touch of a hand on the lock.

And the rustle of a skirt at the very door.

[pause-2]

Someone is standing stealthily close to the lock.

And just as he is doing on the outside, is secretly listening within.

[pause-3]

He moves a little on purpose and mutters something aloud.

So that he might not have the appearance of hiding.

Then rings a third time.

Quietly, soberly, and without impatience.

[pause-2]

Recalling it afterward, that moment stands out in his mind vividly, distinctly, forever.

He could not make out how he had such cunning.

For his mind was clouded at moments.

And he was almost unconscious of his body.

[pause-3]

An instant later, he hears the latch unfastened.

[pause-5]

# ============================================================
# Chapter 7 - The Murder
# ============================================================

The door opens a tiny crack.

Just as before, two sharp and suspicious eyes stare at him out of the darkness.

[pause-2]

Then Raskolnikov loses his head.

He makes a serious blunder.

[pause-2]

Fearing the old woman would be frightened by their being alone—

And not hoping that the sight of him would disarm her suspicions—

He takes hold of the door and draws it toward him to prevent her from attempting to close it again.

[pause-2]

Seeing this, she does not pull the door back.

But she does not let go of the handle, so that he almost drags her out onto the stairs.

[pause-2]

Seeing that she is standing blocking the doorway—

He advances straight upon her.

She steps back in alarm.

Tries to say something.

But seems unable to speak.

And stares with open eyes at him.

[pause-3]

"Good evening, Alyona Ivanovna," he begins, trying to speak easily.

But his voice will not obey him.

It breaks and shakes.

"I have come... I have brought something... but we'd better come in... to the light."

[pause-2]

And leaving her, he passes straight into the room uninvited.

[pause-2]

The old woman runs after him.

Her tongue is unloosed.

"Good heavens! What is it? Who is it? What do you want?"

[pause-2]

"Why, Alyona Ivanovna, you know me... Raskolnikov..."

"Here, I brought you what I promised the other day..."

[pause-2]

And he holds out the pledge to her.

[pause-3]

The old woman glances at it.

But immediately stares with her eyes fixed on the eyes of her uninvited visitor.

She looks intently, maliciously, and mistrustfully.

[pause-2]

A minute passes.

He even fancies something like a sneer in her eyes.

As though she has already guessed everything.

[pause-2]

He feels that he is losing his head.

That he is almost frightened.

So frightened that if she were to go on staring at him like that—

Without a word for another half minute—

He thinks he would have run away from her.

[pause-3]

"Why do you look at me as though you did not know me?" he says suddenly, also with malice.

"Take it if you like. If not, I'll go elsewhere. I have no time to waste."

[pause-2]

He has not thought of saying this.

It is said of itself.

[pause-3]

The old woman recovers herself.

Her visitor's resolute tone seems to reassure her.

[pause-2]

"But why, my good sir, all of a sudden... What is it?" she asks, looking at the pledge.

"The silver cigarette case I spoke of the other day."

[pause-2]

She holds out her hand.

[pause-2]

"But how pale you are, to be sure... and your hands are trembling!"

"Have you been bathing, or what?"

[pause-2]

"Fever," he answers abruptly.

"You can't help getting pale... if you've nothing to eat."

[pause-2]

He can scarcely articulate the words.

His strength fails him again.

But his answer sounds probable.

The old woman takes the pledge.

[pause-3]

"What is it?" she asks once more, scanning Raskolnikov intently.

And weighing the pledge in her hand.

[pause-2]

"A thing... cigarette case... silver... look at it."

"But it does not seem somehow like silver... How he has wrapped it up."

[pause-2]

Trying to untie the string, she turns to the window, to the light.

For in spite of the evening being so hot, all the windows are shut.

[pause-2]

She turns her back upon him.

For some seconds, she is not paying attention to him.

[pause-2]

He unbuttons his coat.

Frees the axe from the noose.

But does not yet take it out altogether.

Simply holds it in his right hand under the coat.

[pause-2]

His hands are fearfully weak.

He feels them every moment growing more numb and more wooden.

He is afraid he will let the axe slip and fall.

[pause-2]

A sudden giddiness comes over him.

[pause-3]

"But what has he tied it up like this for?" the old woman cries with vexation.

And makes a movement as though to turn to him.

[pause-3]

He has not another instant to lose.

[pause-2]

He pulls the axe quite out.

Swings it with both arms, scarcely conscious of himself.

And almost without effort, almost mechanically—

Brings the blunt side down on her head.

[pause-2]

He seems not to use his own strength in this.

[pause-3]

But as soon as he has once brought the axe down—

His strength returns to him.

[pause-3]

The old woman is bareheaded as always.

Her thin, light hair, streaked with grey, thickly smeared with grease—

Is plaited in a rat's tail and fastened by a broken horn comb.

[pause-2]

As she is short, the blow falls on the very top of her skull.

[pause-2]

She cries out.

But very faintly.

And suddenly sinks in a heap to the floor.

Raising her hands to her head.

[pause-2]

In one hand, she still holds the pledge.

[pause-3]

He strikes again and again.

With the blunt side.

On the top of her head.

[pause-2]

The blood gushes as from an overturned glass.

The body falls back.

[pause-2]

He steps back.

Lets it fall.

And immediately bends over her face.

[pause-2]

She is dead.

Her eyes seem to be starting out of their sockets.

The forehead and the whole face are drawn and contorted convulsively.

[pause-5]

He lays the axe on the floor beside the dead woman.

Feels at once in her pocket.

Taking care to avoid the streaming blood.

[pause-2]

The right pocket.

It is from this pocket she has taken the keys on his last visit.

[pause-2]

He is in full possession of his faculties.

Free from confusion or giddiness.

But his hands are still trembling.

[pause-3]

He remembers afterward that he has been particularly careful.

Trying all the time not to get smeared with blood.

[pause-2]

He pulls out the keys at once.

They are all, as before, in one bunch on a steel ring.

[pause-2]

He runs at once to the bedroom with them.

It is a very small room with a whole case of holy images.

And opposite, a big bed.

Very clean with a silk patchwork quilt.

[pause-2]

Against a third wall is a chest of drawers.

[pause-3]

Strange to say, he begins trying to fit the keys into the chest of drawers.

But they do not fit.

This is not so much from his hands trembling—

But from his making continual mistakes.

[pause-2]

He knows, for instance, that a key is not the right one.

That it will not fit.

Yet he persists in trying it.

[pause-3]

Suddenly, he remembers that the big key with the deep notches—

Can certainly not belong to the chest of drawers.

But to some trunk.

[pause-2]

And in that trunk is perhaps everything hidden.

He leaves the chest at once and feels under the bed.

Knowing that old women usually keep boxes under their beds.

[pause-3]

And so it is.

[pause-2]

There is a good-sized box under the bed.

At least a yard in length.

With an arched lid covered with red leather and studded with steel nails.

[pause-2]

The notched key fits at once and unlocks it.

[pause-3]

On the top, under a white sheet, is a coat of red rabbit skin trimmed with rabbit.

Beneath it, a silk dress.

Then a shawl.

And it seems as though there is nothing below but clothes.

[pause-2]

First, he begins wiping his blood-stained hands on the red rabbit skin.

"It's red, and blood will be less noticeable on the red," he reasons.

Then he suddenly comes to himself.

[pause-2]

"Good God, am I going out of my senses?" he thinks with terror.

[pause-3]

But no sooner does he touch the clothes than a gold watch slips from under the fur coat.

He makes haste to turn them all over.

[pause-2]

There are gold objects among the clothes—

All probably pledges, unredeemed or waiting to be redeemed.

Bracelets, chains, earrings, pins, and so forth.

[pause-2]

Some are in cases.

Others simply wrapped in newspaper.

Carefully and neatly folded.

Tied with tape.

[pause-2]

Without any delay, he begins filling the pockets of his trousers and his overcoat.

Without examining or undoing the parcels and cases.

[pause-2]

But he does not have time to take many.

[pause-5]

Suddenly, he hears steps in the room where the old woman lies.

[pause-2]

He stops short and is still as death.

[pause-2]

But all is quiet.

So it must have been his fancy.

[pause-2]

Then he distinctly hears a faint cry.

Or rather a weak, broken groan.

[pause-2]

Then dead silence again for a minute or two.

[pause-3]

He sits squatting on his heels by the box.

Waiting.

Hardly breathing.

[pause-2]

Then he jumps up.

Seizes the axe.

And runs out of the bedroom.

[pause-5]

In the middle of the room stands Lizaveta.

With a big bundle in her arms.

[pause-2]

She is gazing in stupefaction at her murdered sister.

White as a sheet.

Seeming not to have the strength to cry out.

[pause-2]

Seeing him run out of the bedroom, she begins faintly quivering all over.

Like a leaf.

A shudder runs down her face.

[pause-2]

She lifts her hand.

Opens her mouth.

But still does not scream.

[pause-2]

She begins slowly backing away from him into the corner.

Staring intently, persistently at him.

But still not uttering a sound.

[pause-2]

As though she has no breath left to cry out.

[pause-3]

He rushes at her with the axe.

Her mouth twitches piteously.

As one sees babies' mouths when they begin to be frightened.

When they stare at what frightens them.

And are on the point of screaming.

[pause-2]

And this hapless Lizaveta is so simple, so thoroughly crushed, so frightened—

That she does not even raise a hand to guard her face.

Though that is the most necessary and natural action at the moment.

For the axe is raised over her face.

[pause-3]

She only puts up her empty left hand.

But not to her face.

Slowly holding it out before her as though motioning him away.

[pause-3]

The axe falls—with the sharp edge this time—directly on the skull.

It splits at one blow all the top of the head.

[pause-3]

She falls heavily at once.

[pause-2]

Raskolnikov loses all presence of mind.

[pause-2]

Snatching up her bundle, he drops it again.

Runs into the entry.

[pause-3]

Fear gains more and more mastery over him.

Especially after this second, quite unexpected murder.

[pause-2]

He longs to run away from the place as fast as possible.

[pause-2]

And if at that moment he had been capable of seeing and reasoning more correctly—

If he had been able to realize all the difficulties of his position—

The hopelessness, the hideousness, and the absurdity of it—

If he could have understood how many obstacles and perhaps crimes he had still to overcome—

To get out of that place and to make his way home—

It is very possible he would have flung up everything and would have gone to give himself up.

[pause-2]

Not from fear, but from simple horror and loathing of what he has done.

The feeling of loathing rises and grows stronger with every moment.

Not for all the world would he go back to the box or even into that room.

[pause-5]

But a sort of blankness, almost an absent-mindedness, begins by degrees to take possession of him.

At moments, he forgets himself.

Or rather, forgets what is important and clings to trifles.

[pause-3]

Glancing into the kitchen, he sees a bucket half full of water on a bench.

He begins washing his hands and the axe.

[pause-2]

His hands are sticky with blood.

He drops the axe with the blade in the water.

Snatches up a piece of soap that lies in a broken saucer on the window.

And begins washing his hands in the bucket.

[pause-2]

When they are clean, he draws out the axe.

Washes the blade.

Spends a long time—some three minutes—washing the wood where there are spots of blood.

Rubbing it with soap.

[pause-2]

Then he wipes it all with some linen that is hanging to dry on a line in the kitchen.

And examines the axe for a long time at the window.

[pause-2]

There is no trace left on it.

Only the wood is still damp.

[pause-2]

He carefully puts the axe in the noose under his coat.

Then, as far as is possible, in the dim light in the kitchen, he looks over his overcoat, his trousers, his boots.

[pause-2]

At first glance, there seems to be nothing but stains on the boots.

He wets the rag and rubs the boots.

But he knows he is not looking thoroughly.

That there may be something quite noticeable that he is missing.

[pause-3]

He stands in the middle of the room, lost in thought.

Dark, agonizing ideas rise in his mind.

The idea that he is mad and that at this moment he is incapable of thinking.

Of protecting himself.

That he ought perhaps to be doing something utterly different from what he is doing.

[pause-3]

"Good God!" he mutters. "I must run away, I must run away!"

[pause-2]

He rushes back to the entry.

[pause-2]

There, more terrible terror awaits him than any he has known before.

[pause-5]

# The Escape
# ============================================================

He stands in the passage, listening.

[pause-2]

Someone below has opened the street door.

And is coming up, shouting something.

[pause-3]

"What is it, are they all coming?" flashes through his mind.

[pause-2]

He fastens the door with the latch.

"But no—they might be heard!"

[pause-2]

Then his arm grows numb.

He feels he will drop.

Someone is mounting the staircase.

Coming to the fourth floor.

[pause-3]

But already he hears that step.

He knows it at once.

It is someone coming there.

To the fourth floor.

To the door.

To this flat.

[pause-3]

He is petrified as in a dream.

When one dreams that one is pursued and closely followed.

They are going to kill one.

And one is rooted to the spot.

Cannot even move one's arms.

[pause-3]

At last, the visitor reaches the fourth floor.

[pause-2]

He stops at Alyona Ivanovna's door.

Opposite.

[pause-3]

"Who's there?" calls a loud, resolute voice.

[pause-5]

Raskolnikov holds his breath.

[pause-2]

The visitor gives the bell a violent pull.

The bell tinkles sharply.

[pause-2]

A few seconds later, he gives another ring.

Louder still.

[pause-2]

Then waits, loses patience, and begins shaking the handle with all his might.

[pause-3]

Raskolnikov looks in horror at the hook shaking in its fastening.

In blank terror, the thought that the latch might slip out flashes through his mind.

That he might easily hold it—

But then he would know someone is inside.

[pause-2]

His head seems whirling.

He is almost falling.

[pause-3]

"What the devil is the matter?" the visitor cries suddenly in exasperation.

[pause-2]

He leaves the door, but immediately seizes the bell again.

Gives it another hard ring.

[pause-3]

Then another ring.

And a third.

[pause-5]

A second visitor arrives.

[pause-2]

"Is she asleep or murdered?" this new voice says harshly.

"E-eh! Alyona Ivanovna, old witch! Are you alive or not? Open the door!"

[pause-2]

"What is it?" the first visitor cries, now alarmed.

"I've been ringing half an hour."

"Very strange."

"She never goes anywhere."

[pause-3]

"Hm!... The door is fastened from inside. That means someone is at home."

"I tell you what: go down to the porter. Let him come up."

[pause-2]

One of them runs downstairs.

[pause-2]

The other remains.

He gives the bell one more tug.

Then he too goes down.

Not hurrying.

Thumping with heavy boots on the stairs.

[pause-5]

Raskolnikov takes off the latch and opens the door—

He can hear nothing.

[pause-2]

Suddenly, without thinking, he goes out.

Closes the door behind him as best he can.

And walks down the stairs.

[pause-3]

He has already gone down three flights when a violent commotion rises below.

Where can he hide?

[pause-2]

There is nowhere.

[pause-2]

He runs back to the flat again.

[pause-2]

"Hey there! Catch the devil!"

[pause-2]

Someone dashes out of a flat below.

Shouting.

Rather falls than runs down the stairs.

[pause-2]

"Mitka! Mitka! Mitka! Blast you!"

[pause-2]

The shout ends in a shriek.

The last sounds reach him from the courtyard.

All is still.

[pause-5]

But at that instant, several men talking fast and loud—there seem to be three or four—

Begin mounting the stairs noisily.

[pause-3]

Raskolnikov's mind goes blank.

[pause-2]

Then suddenly—

[pause-2]

He notices the door of the flat that stood open—

The flat on the second floor where the painters have been working.

It is closed now.

But a thin streak of light shows under the door.

[pause-2]

The painters have left.

No one is there.

[pause-3]

By instinct, he darts in and hides behind the wall.

[pause-2]

And only just in time.

They are already on that very landing.

They turn and go on up to the fourth floor, talking loudly.

[pause-3]

He waits.

Goes out on tiptoe.

Runs down the stairs.

[pause-2]

No one on the stairs.

In the gateway—no one.

[pause-2]

He passes quickly through the gate.

Turns to the left in the street.

[pause-3]

He knows very well that they are now in the flat.

That they are amazed at finding it open, when it was closed just now.

That they are looking at the bodies.

[pause-2]

He knows that in another moment some of them will come out.

[pause-2]

He knows he must not run.

He turns into the first turning.

[pause-5]

He is scarcely breathing.

[pause-3]

He crosses two turnings.

Then turns into another street.

[pause-2]

"Yes, into an alley. Better so."

[pause-2]

He is walking more calmly now.

To all appearance, a man a little the worse for drink.

[pause-5]

His thoughts wander.

[pause-2]

He remembers: he meant to throw the axe in the canal.

But now—he is going home, not to the canal.

[pause-2]

He must put back the axe at once.

Yes, put it back.

[pause-3]

Instead of going to the gate, he enters from the other side.

Approaches the back stairs.

And mounts them.

[pause-2]

The porter's door is open.

He goes in.

Listens.

[pause-2]

Eyes shut, not daring to look.

He takes out the axe from under his coat.

Puts it on the spot where it lay before, under the bench.

[pause-2]

He even covers it with the wood as before.

[pause-3]

He meets no one.

Not a soul.

[pause-2]

He walks up to his room.

Opens the door.

Goes in.

[pause-5]

Without undressing, he throws himself on the sofa.

[pause-2]

He does not sleep.

But sinks into blank forgetfulness.

[pause-2]

If anyone had come into his room then—

He would have jumped up at once and screamed.

[pause-3]

Scraps and shreds of thoughts are swarming in his brain.

But he cannot catch at one.

Cannot dwell upon one.

[pause-2]

In spite of all his efforts.

So.

It is done.

[pause-3]

What strikes me most about this chapter is the body.

[pause-2]

Not the old woman's body.

Raskolnikov's body.

[pause-3]

His hands tremble so badly he can barely hold the axe.

His voice breaks and shakes.

His heart pounds until he cannot breathe.

He forgets himself, clings to trifles.

Washes his hands obsessively.

Runs when he should walk.

Hides when he should escape.

[pause-2]

The theory said he was extraordinary.

A Napoleon.

One of those who have the right to transgress.

[pause-2]

But his body knows the truth.

His body is in revolt.

[pause-3]

And then there is Lizaveta.

[pause-2]

She was not in the plan.

She was supposed to be away.

Seven o'clock.

The coincidence.

The sign.

[pause-2]

But she came back.

With her bundle.

Her simple, frightened face.

Her mouth twitching like a baby about to cry.

[pause-3]

She does not even raise her hand to shield herself.

She only holds it out before her, as though motioning him away.

[pause-2]

And he kills her anyway.

[pause-3]

This is what the theory cannot account for.

[pause-2]

The old woman was a louse—he told himself.

Worthless.

Harmful.

Better off dead.

[pause-2]

But Lizaveta?

[pause-2]

Lizaveta was gentle.

Kind.

A victim herself.

The student in the tavern said she had a sweet smile.

That lots of people were attracted to her.

[pause-3]

She was exactly the kind of person Raskolnikov's theory was supposed to save.

One of the thousands who could be helped with the old woman's money.

[pause-2]

And he killed her.

[pause-2]

Because she was there.

Because she saw.

Because once you cross that line, there are no more limits.

[pause-3]

This is Dostoevsky's answer to the utilitarian calculus.

[pause-2]

One death for a hundred lives?

Simple arithmetic?

[pause-2]

But murder is not arithmetic.

Murder is chaos.

Murder creates more murder.

[pause-3]

Raskolnikov went to kill one person.

He killed two.

[pause-2]

And he took nothing.

A few trinkets in his pockets.

He never even opens the purse he stole.

He will bury it all under a stone and never return for it.

[pause-3]

The theory promised power.

Freedom.

The right to step over.

[pause-2]

What he got was this:

Lying on his sofa in blank forgetfulness.

Scraps and shreds of thoughts swarming in his brain.

Unable to catch at one.

Unable to rest.

The murder took perhaps fifteen minutes.

The punishment will last the rest of the novel.

And the rest of his life.
