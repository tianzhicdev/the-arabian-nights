# Crime and Punishment: Episode 2 - "The Letter and the Theory"
# Narrated by Celeste
# Runtime: Approximately 30-35 minutes

# ============================================================
# HIGHLIGHT (30 seconds - 1 minute)
# ============================================================

"Never such a marriage while I am alive, and Mr. Luzhin be damned!"

His mother writes of duty.

His sister speaks of sacrifice.

But Raskolnikov sees it clearly now.

They are selling her.

Just as Sonia was sold.

For him.

[pause-5]

# ============================================================
# INTRODUCTION
# ============================================================

This is Celeste, telling you the story from Crime and Punishment.

[pause-3]

# ============================================================
# BRIEF RECAP
# ============================================================

Last time, we met Raskolnikov—a former law student living in a coffin of a room in St. Petersburg.

We watched him make his "rehearsal" visit to an old pawnbroker, memorizing the layout, asking about her sister.

And we sat with him in a tavern as a ruined man named Marmeladov confessed the unthinkable—

How his daughter Sonia became a prostitute to feed her starving family.

[pause-2]

Today, a letter arrives.

And everything changes.

# ============================================================
# PART ONE: THE STORY
# Chapter 3 - The Room and the Letter
# ============================================================

[pause-3]

He wakes late the next day after broken sleep.

The sleep has not refreshed him.

He is bilious, irritable, ill-tempered.

And he looks at his room with hatred.

[pause-2]

Let me describe this room again, because Dostoevsky wants us to feel it.

A tiny cupboard of a space, about six paces in length.

Dusty yellow wallpaper peeling off the walls.

So low-pitched that a man of average height feels he might knock his head on the ceiling.

[pause-2]

Three rickety chairs.

A painted table with dusty manuscripts and books—untouched for weeks.

And a big clumsy sofa, once covered with chintz, now in rags.

This is where Raskolnikov sleeps, wrapped in his old student overcoat, without sheets.

[pause-2]

He has withdrawn completely.

Like a tortoise in its shell, Dostoevsky says.

Even the sight of the servant girl makes him writhe with irritation.

[pause-3]

His landlady has stopped sending him meals.

He has not thought to complain.

Nastasya, the cook, has stopped cleaning his room.

Only once a week does she stray in with a broom.

[pause-2]

This morning, she wakes him.

"Get up, why are you asleep! It's past nine. I've brought you some tea. Will you have a cup?"

"I should think you're fairly starving."

[pause-2]

He opens his eyes, recognizes her, asks weakly:

"From the landlady?"

"From the landlady, indeed!"

[pause-2]

It's her own tea.

Her own cracked teapot.

Two yellow lumps of sugar.

[pause-3]

She sits beside him and begins to chatter.

She is a country woman, talkative, not unkind.

"Praskovya Pavlovna means to complain to the police about you."

[pause-2]

He scowls.

"To the police? What does she want?"

"You don't pay her money and you won't turn out of the room. That's what she wants."

[pause-2]

"The devil, that's the last straw," he mutters, grinding his teeth.

"No, that would not suit me... just now."

[pause-2]

Just now.

Remember that phrase.

He has plans.

The landlady's threats cannot be allowed to interfere.

[pause-3]

Nastasya presses him.

"Why, if you're so clever, do you lie here like a sack and have nothing to show for it?"

"One time you used to go out to teach children. But why is it you do nothing now?"

[pause-2]

"I am doing..." he begins, reluctant.

"What are you doing?"

[pause-2]

"Work."

"What sort of work?"

[pause-2]

"I am thinking."

[pause-3]

Nastasya bursts out laughing.

She laughs until she shakes, until she feels ill.

"And have you made much money by your thinking?"

[pause-2]

"One can't go out to give lessons without boots. And I'm sick of it."

"Don't quarrel with your bread and butter."

"They pay so little for lessons. What's the use of a few coppers?"

[pause-2]

"And you want to get a fortune all at once?"

[pause-2]

He looks at her strangely.

"Yes. I want a fortune."

[pause-3]

Then—the letter.

"Ah, I forgot! A letter came for you yesterday when you were out."

"A letter? For me! From whom?"

"I can't say. I gave three copecks of my own to the postman for it. Will you pay me back?"

[pause-2]

"Then bring it to me, for God's sake, bring it!" he cries, suddenly excited.

[pause-2]

A minute later, she returns with the letter.

His hands tremble as he takes it.

It is from his mother.

From the province of R——.

[pause-2]

He has not received a letter in a long time.

But another feeling stabs his heart as he holds it.

Something like dread.

[pause-2]

"Nastasya, leave me alone, for goodness' sake. Here are your three copecks, but for goodness' sake, make haste and go!"

[pause-2]

The letter quivers in his hand.

He does not want to open it in her presence.

He wants to be alone with it.

[pause-3]

When she goes, he lifts it to his lips and kisses it.

Then gazes at the address.

The small, sloping handwriting.

So dear and familiar.

The hand of the mother who once taught him to read and write.

[pause-2]

He delays.

He seems almost afraid.

[pause-2]

At last, he opens it.

It is thick.

Heavy.

Two large sheets covered with very small handwriting.

[pause-5]

# ============================================================
# The Mother's Letter
# ============================================================

"My dear Rodya," his mother writes.

"It's two months since I last had a talk with you by letter, which has distressed me and even kept me awake at night."

"But I am sure you will not blame me for my inevitable silence."

"You know how I love you."

"You are all we have to look to, Dounia and I."

"You are our all, our one hope, our one stay."

[pause-3]

She tells him she knows he left the university months ago for want of money.

That he lost his lessons.

That he is struggling.

[pause-2]

"How could I help you out of my hundred and twenty roubles a year pension?"

"The fifteen roubles I sent you four months ago I borrowed on security of my pension."

"I had to wait till the debt was paid off before I could send more."

[pause-2]

But now, she says, things have changed.

Now she has news.

[pause-3]

"In the first place, would you have guessed, dear Rodya, that your sister has been living with me for the last six weeks?"

"We shall not be separated in the future."

"Thank God, her sufferings are over."

[pause-2]

Her sufferings.

[pause-3]

The mother tells the story carefully.

Too carefully, perhaps.

[pause-2]

Dounia had been working as a governess for a family called the Svidrigailovs.

Mr. Svidrigailov—the master of the house—had treated her rudely at first.

Made disrespectful remarks.

Sneered at her.

[pause-2]

But it was worse than rudeness, she admits.

Much worse.

[pause-2]

"Would you believe that the crazy fellow had conceived a passion for Dounia from the beginning?"

"But had concealed it under a show of rudeness and contempt."

"Possibly he was ashamed of his own feelings, considering his years and his being a father of a family."

"And that made him angry with Dounia."

[pause-3]

"At last he lost all control."

"He made her an open and shameful proposal."

"Promised her all sorts of inducements."

"Offered to throw up everything and take her to another estate, or even abroad."

[pause-2]

Dounia could not leave.

She had taken a hundred roubles in advance—money she had used to send sixty roubles to Raskolnikov.

She was bound by debt.

[pause-2]

And she could not speak of it without arousing Mrs. Svidrigailova's suspicions.

Without causing scandal.

Without destroying herself.

[pause-3]

"You know your sister, Rodya."

"You know how clever she is and what a strong will she has."

"Dounia can endure a great deal."

"Even in the most difficult cases, she has the fortitude to maintain her firmness."

[pause-2]

She endured.

For weeks.

Alone.

[pause-3]

Then the catastrophe.

[pause-2]

Mrs. Svidrigailova—Marfa Petrovna—overheard her husband imploring Dounia in the garden.

She put the wrong interpretation on what she saw.

She blamed Dounia.

[pause-2]

"An awful scene took place between them on the spot."

"Marfa Petrovna went so far as to strike Dounia."

"Refused to hear anything."

"Was shouting at her for a whole hour."

"Then gave orders that Dounia should be packed off at once in a plain peasant's cart."

[pause-2]

They flung all her things into the cart—her linen, her clothes—all pell-mell, without folding, without packing.

And a heavy rain was falling.

[pause-2]

"And Dounia, insulted and put to shame, had to drive with a peasant in an open cart all the seventeen versts into town."

[pause-5]

Raskolnikov reads this, and his face grows pale.

[pause-3]

The mother continues.

[pause-2]

For a whole month, the town was full of gossip.

Dounia and her mother could not even go to church.

Contemptuous looks.

Whispers.

Remarks made aloud.

[pause-2]

Their acquaintances avoided them.

Nobody bowed to them in the street.

Shopmen and clerks were planning to smear their gates with pitch.

The landlord told them they must leave.

[pause-3]

All this was Marfa Petrovna's doing.

She knew everyone in the district.

She told everyone her version.

[pause-2]

"It made me ill," the mother writes.

"But Dounia bore it better than I did."

"If only you could have seen how she endured it all and tried to comfort me and cheer me up!"

"She is an angel."

[pause-3]

Then—a reversal.

[pause-2]

Mr. Svidrigailov, apparently returning to his senses, gave his wife proof of Dounia's innocence.

A letter Dounia had written, refusing his advances.

Reproaching him.

Telling him how infamous his behavior was.

[pause-2]

Marfa Petrovna was "completely crushed."

She went straight to the Cathedral, prayed on her knees.

Then came to Dounia's lodgings, weeping, begging forgiveness.

[pause-2]

And then—in the way of such women—she went to every house in the town.

Shedding tears.

Reading Dounia's letter aloud.

Showing everyone.

[pause-2]

"In this way she was busy for several days, driving about the whole town."

"Some people had taken offense through precedence having been given to others."

"So they had to take turns."

[pause-2]

A grotesque image.

The whole town, assembled in their parlors, waiting their turn to hear the letter that would restore Dounia's honor.

[pause-3]

But now, the mother says, comes the truly important news.

[pause-2]

Dounia has a suitor.

She has already consented to marry him.

[pause-3]

# ============================================================
# The Suitor
# ============================================================

His name is Pyotr Petrovitch Luzhin.

He is a counselor.

A man of business.

Forty-five years old.

[pause-2]

"He is a well-to-do man, to be depended upon."

"He has two posts in the government and has already made his fortune."

"It is true that he is forty-five years old."

"But he is of a fairly prepossessing appearance."

"And he is altogether a very respectable and presentable man."

"Only he seems a little morose and somewhat conceited."

[pause-2]

The mother hastens to add:

"But possibly that may only be the impression he makes at first sight."

[pause-3]

Raskolnikov reads on, his jaw tightening.

[pause-2]

Luzhin, she explains, is a "practical man."

He shares the ideas of "our most rising generation."

He is an opponent of all prejudices.

[pause-2]

"He said a good deal more, for he seems a little conceited and likes to be listened to."

"But this is scarcely a vice."

[pause-2]

The mother admits she understood very little of what he said.

But Dounia explained that he is clever and seems to be good-natured.

[pause-3]

Then—a detail that makes Raskolnikov's blood boil.

[pause-2]

"At his second visit, after he had received Dounia's consent, in the course of conversation, he declared—"

[pause-2]

"That before making Dounia's acquaintance, he had made up his mind to marry a girl of good reputation, without dowry."

"And above all, one who had experienced poverty."

[pause-2]

"Because, as he explained—"

"A man ought not to be indebted to his wife."

"It is better for a wife to look upon her husband as her benefactor."

[pause-3]

Raskolnikov stops reading.

He reads that passage again.

[pause-2]

A man ought not to be indebted to his wife.

It is better for a wife to look upon her husband as her benefactor.

[pause-2]

This is what Luzhin wants.

A wife who will be grateful.

A wife who will owe him everything.

A wife who will be—in essence—bought.

[pause-3]

The mother seems uneasy about this too.

"It did strike me as somewhat rude," she admits.

"And I said so afterwards to Dounia."

[pause-2]

"But Dounia was vexed, and answered that 'words are not deeds.'"

"And that, of course, is perfectly true."

[pause-2]

Raskolnikov can hear his sister's voice in that response.

Practical.

Resigned.

Determined to see it through.

[pause-3]

The mother continues with plans.

[pause-2]

Luzhin is going to Petersburg to open a legal bureau.

He has a case before the Senate.

He could be of great use to Raskolnikov—perhaps even employ him.

[pause-2]

"Dounia is dreaming of nothing else."

"She has even ventured to speak to Pyotr Petrovitch about it."

"He was cautious in his answer."

"Said that of course he could not get on without a secretary."

"That it would be better to pay a salary to a relation than to a stranger."

"If only the former were fitted for the duties."

[pause-2]

If only.

[pause-3]

Dounia has a regular plan, the mother says.

Raskolnikov will become Luzhin's associate.

Eventually, his partner.

His future is assured.

[pause-2]

"If only this comes to pass! This would be such a benefit that we could only look upon it as a providential blessing."

[pause-3]

The letter nears its end.

[pause-2]

They are coming to Petersburg.

Soon.

Perhaps in a week.

[pause-2]

The wedding will be quick—Luzhin is anxious to have it done.

Before the fast of Our Lady, if possible.

Or immediately after.

[pause-2]

"Oh, with what happiness I shall press you to my heart!"

"Dounia is all excitement at the joyful thought of seeing you."

"She said one day in joke that she would be ready to marry Pyotr Petrovitch for that alone."

[pause-2]

A joke.

[pause-3]

The mother mentions travel arrangements.

Luzhin has offered to convey their bags and trunk through some acquaintances.

But they will travel themselves by peasant cart to the railway, then third class the rest of the way.

[pause-2]

"So I may very likely be able to send you not twenty-five, but thirty roubles."

[pause-2]

Thirty roubles.

From a woman who has nothing.

For a son she adores.

[pause-3]

The letter closes:

[pause-2]

"Love Dounia your sister, Rodya. Love her as she loves you."

"And understand that she loves you beyond everything, more than herself."

"She is an angel, and you, Rodya, you are everything to us."

"Our one hope, our one consolation."

"If only you are happy, we shall be happy."

[pause-2]

"Do you still say your prayers, Rodya, and believe in the mercy of our Creator and our Redeemer?"

"I am afraid in my heart that you may have been visited by the new spirit of infidelity that is abroad today."

"If it is so, I pray for you."

[pause-2]

"Good-bye, till we meet. I embrace you warmly, warmly, with many kisses."

"Yours till death,"

"Pulcheria Raskolnikov."

[pause-5]

# ============================================================
# Chapter 4 - Raskolnikov's Reaction
# ============================================================

Almost from the first, while he reads, Raskolnikov's face is wet with tears.

But when he finishes—

His face is pale and distorted.

A bitter, wrathful, malignant smile is on his lips.

[pause-3]

He lays his head on his threadbare pillow.

And ponders.

A long time.

[pause-2]

His heart beats violently.

His brain is in a turmoil.

[pause-2]

At last he feels cramped and stifled in the little yellow room.

His eyes and his mind crave space.

[pause-2]

He takes up his hat and goes out.

This time without dread of meeting anyone.

He has forgotten his dread.

[pause-3]

He turns toward Vassilyevsky Ostrov.

Walking as his habit is, without noticing the way.

Muttering aloud.

Speaking to himself.

Passers-by take him for a drunk.

[pause-3]

And here is what he is thinking.

[pause-2]

"Never such a marriage while I am alive, and Mr. Luzhin be damned!"

[pause-2]

"The thing is perfectly clear."

"No, mother, no, Dounia, you won't deceive me!"

"And then they apologize for not asking my advice, for taking the decision without me!"

"I dare say!"

"They imagine it is arranged now and can't be broken off."

"But we will see whether it can or not!"

[pause-3]

He sees through it all.

[pause-2]

Luzhin's "practical" philosophy—wanting a wife who has "experienced poverty."

A wife who will look upon him as her benefactor.

A wife who will be indebted.

[pause-2]

And his mother's careful optimism.

Her insistence that Luzhin is kind, that he seems good-natured.

Her willingness to overlook the warning signs.

[pause-3]

"Why does she write to me about 'our most rising generation'?"

"Simply as a descriptive touch, or with the idea of prepossessing me in favor of Mr. Luzhin?"

"Oh, the cunning of them!"

[pause-2]

But beneath his anger is something else.

Something like despair.

[pause-2]

"And what if I am wrong?" he thinks.

"What can you do to prevent it?"

"What right have you?"

"What can you promise them to give you such a right?"

[pause-2]

"Your whole life, your whole future, you will devote to them—when you have finished your studies and obtained a post?"

"Yes, we have heard all that before, and that's all words."

"But now? Now something must be done, now, do you understand that?"

[pause-2]

"And what are you doing now?"

"You are living upon them."

"They borrow on their hundred roubles pension."

"They borrow from the Svidrigailovs."

[pause-3]

He is trapped.

[pause-2]

He cannot stop the marriage because he has nothing to offer instead.

No money.

No prospects.

No way to support them.

[pause-2]

And Dounia is selling herself—for him.

[pause-3]

He thinks of Sonia.

The girl in Marmeladov's story.

The girl who put on her kerchief and went out at six o'clock and came back at nine with thirty roubles.

[pause-2]

"Sonia! Sonia Marmeladov, the eternal victim so long as the world lasts!"

[pause-2]

"Have you taken the measure of your sacrifice, both of you?"

"Is it right? Can you bear it? Is it any use? Is there sense in it?"

[pause-2]

"And let me tell you, Dounia—Sonia's life is no worse than life with Mr. Luzhin."

[pause-3]

That is the connection he makes.

[pause-2]

His sister, marrying a man who wants a grateful, indebted wife.

Sonia, selling herself in the streets.

[pause-2]

Both are transactions.

Both are women being bought.

Both are sacrifices made for family.

[pause-3]

"It has to be paid for, Dounia, this smartness."

"And what if it's more than you can bear afterwards, if you regret it?"

"The bitterness, the misery, the curses, the tears hidden from all the world—"

[pause-2]

"And how will your mother feel then?"

"Even now she is uneasy, she is worried."

"But then, when she sees it all clearly?"

[pause-2]

"And I?"

[pause-2]

"Yes, indeed, what have you taken me for?"

"I won't have your sacrifice, Dounia, I won't have it, mother!"

"It shall not be, so long as I am alive, it shall not, it shall not!"

[pause-3]

He pauses in the street.

[pause-2]

"It shall not be? But what are you going to do to prevent it?"

"You'll forbid it?"

"And what right have you?"

[pause-2]

A terrible question.

[pause-2]

"What can you promise them on your side to give you such a right?"

"Your whole life, your whole future, you will devote to them—"

"When you have finished your studies and obtained a post?"

[pause-2]

"Yes, we have heard all that before."

"But now?"

"Now something must be done."

"Now."

[pause-3]

And here it comes.

The thought he has been circling around.

The idea that has been growing in his coffin-room for a month.

[pause-2]

"Or throw up life altogether!" he cries suddenly, in a frenzy.

"Accept one's lot humbly as it is, once for all—"

"And stifle everything in oneself, giving up all claim to activity, life, and love!"

[pause-3]

Marmeladov's question echoes in his mind.

[pause-2]

"Do you understand, sir, do you understand what it means when you have absolutely nowhere to turn?"

"For every man must have somewhere to turn..."

[pause-3]

He gives a sudden start.

[pause-2]

Another thought has slipped back into his mind.

A thought he had yesterday.

[pause-2]

But he knew it would come back.

He was expecting it.

It was not only yesterday's thought.

[pause-2]

The difference is that a month ago, yesterday even, the thought was a mere dream.

But now—

Now it appears not a dream at all.

It has taken a new menacing shape.

Quite unfamiliar.

[pause-2]

And he suddenly becomes aware of this himself.

[pause-3]

He feels a hammering in his head.

There is a darkness before his eyes.

[pause-2]

He looks around hurriedly, searching for a seat.

He needs to sit down.

[pause-2]

There is a bench about a hundred paces ahead.

He walks toward it as fast as he can.

[pause-5]

But on the way, something distracts him.

[pause-2]

A girl.

Perhaps fifteen or sixteen.

Walking in the great heat, bareheaded, no parasol, waving her arms absurdly.

[pause-2]

Her dress is torn, put on strangely, not properly fastened.

She is stumbling and staggering.

[pause-2]

She reaches the bench and drops onto it.

Her head sinks back.

Her eyes close.

[pause-2]

She is completely drunk.

[pause-3]

Raskolnikov stares.

She has a pretty face—flushed, swollen, but pretty.

Fair-haired.

Young.

So young.

[pause-2]

She has been violated.

That is obvious.

Given drink and deceived somewhere.

For the first time, probably.

And put out into the street.

[pause-3]

And on the other side of the boulevard—a gentleman.

Plump.

Thickly-set.

Fashionably dressed.

Red lips and moustaches.

[pause-2]

He has been following her.

Waiting.

[pause-2]

Raskolnikov understands immediately.

[pause-3]

He approaches the gentleman.

"Hey! You Svidrigailov! What do you want here?"

[pause-2]

He doesn't know why he uses that name.

The man in his mother's letter.

The predator.

[pause-2]

"What do you mean?" the gentleman asks, scowling.

"Get away, that's what I mean."

"How dare you, you low fellow!"

[pause-2]

The man raises his cane.

Raskolnikov rushes at him with his fists.

[pause-2]

But a policeman separates them.

[pause-3]

Raskolnikov explains quickly.

The girl is drunk.

She's been taken advantage of.

That man is following her.

He should not be allowed to have her.

[pause-2]

The policeman examines the girl.

His face works with genuine compassion.

"Ah, what a pity. She is quite a child! She has been deceived, you can see that at once."

[pause-2]

Raskolnikov gives the policeman twenty kopecks.

"Call a cab and take her home."

[pause-3]

But then—suddenly—something changes.

[pause-2]

"Hey, here!" he shouts after the policeman.

The man turns.

[pause-2]

"Let them be! What is it to do with you? Let her go! Let him amuse himself."

He points at the gentleman.

"What is it to do with you?"

[pause-2]

The policeman stares, bewildered.

[pause-2]

Raskolnikov laughs.

[pause-2]

"Well!" the policeman says with contempt, and walks off—probably taking Raskolnikov for a madman.

[pause-3]

Raskolnikov is left alone on the boulevard.

[pause-2]

"He has carried off my twenty kopecks," he mutters.

"Well, let him take as much from the other fellow to allow him to have the girl, and so let it end."

[pause-2]

"And why did I want to interfere? Is it for me to help? Have I any right to help?"

"Let them devour each other alive—what is it to me?"

"How did I dare give him twenty kopecks? Were they mine?"

[pause-5]

He sits on the bench.

His thoughts stray aimlessly.

He cannot fix his mind on anything.

[pause-2]

He longs to forget himself altogether.

To forget everything.

And then to wake up and begin life anew.

[pause-3]

"Poor girl," he says, looking at the empty corner where she sat.

"She will come to herself and weep, and then her mother will find out."

"She will give her a beating, a horrible, shameful beating."

"And then maybe turn her out of doors."

[pause-2]

"And then—the hospital."

"And then—more drink."

"More taverns."

"More hospital."

"And in two or three years—a wreck. Her life over at eighteen or nineteen."

[pause-2]

"Have I not seen cases like that?"

"And how have they been brought to it?"

[pause-2]

"Why, they've all come to it like that."

[pause-3]

Then something bitter, something almost scientific:

[pause-2]

"A certain percentage, they tell us, must every year go that way."

"To the devil, I suppose."

"So that the rest may remain chaste, and not be interfered with."

[pause-2]

"A percentage! What splendid words they have; they are so scientific, so consolatory."

"Once you've said 'percentage,' there's nothing more to worry about."

"If we had any other word... maybe we might feel more uneasy."

[pause-3]

"And what if Dounia were one of the percentage?"

"Of another one, if not that one?"

[pause-5]

He walks on.

Thinking of his friend Razumihin.

Perhaps he could help.

Perhaps he could get Raskolnikov some lessons, some work.

[pause-2]

But no.

What good would that do?

A few kopecks here and there.

It wouldn't change anything.

[pause-3]

"Could I have expected to set it all straight by means of Razumihin alone?" he asks himself.

[pause-2]

He ponders.

Rubs his forehead.

[pause-2]

And then—strangely—as if by chance—a fantastic thought comes into his head.

[pause-3]

"Hm... to Razumihin's," he says, suddenly calm.

"I shall go to Razumihin's, of course."

"But—not now."

[pause-2]

"I shall go to him... on the next day after It."

"When It will be over and everything will begin afresh."

[pause-3]

And suddenly he realizes what he is thinking.

[pause-2]

"After It!" he shouts, jumping up from the seat.

"But is It really going to happen?"

"Is it possible it really will happen?"

[pause-5]

He walks on.

Almost running now.

He means to go home, but the thought of home fills him with loathing.

That hole.

That coffin.

Where all this has been growing in him for a month.

[pause-2]

He walks at random.

A nervous shudder passes into fever.

In spite of the heat, he feels cold.

[pause-2]

He stares at objects, trying to distract himself.

But he keeps dropping into brooding.

When he lifts his head, he forgets what he was just thinking.

He forgets where he is going.

[pause-3]

He walks across Vassilyevsky Ostrov.

Comes out on the Lesser Neva.

Crosses the bridge.

Turns toward the islands.

[pause-2]

The greenness and freshness are restful after the dust of the town.

No taverns.

No stifling closeness.

No stench.

[pause-2]

But soon even these pleasant sensations pass into irritability.

[pause-2]

He stands before a brightly painted summer villa.

Gazes through the fence at smartly dressed women on the verandah.

Children running in the garden.

Flowers.

[pause-2]

He watches them with curious eyes.

And forgets about them before they vanish from sight.

[pause-3]

He counts his money.

Thirty kopecks left.

"Twenty to the policeman, three to Nastasya for the letter..."

[pause-2]

But he soon forgets why he took the money out.

[pause-2]

He goes into a tavern.

Drinks a glass of vodka.

Eats a pie.

[pause-2]

It has been a long time since he had vodka.

It affects him at once.

His legs grow heavy.

A great drowsiness comes over him.

[pause-3]

He turns homeward.

But reaching Petrovsky Island, he stops, exhausted.

Turns off the road into the bushes.

Sinks down upon the grass.

[pause-2]

And instantly falls asleep.

# DISCUSSION (Less than 5 minutes)
# ============================================================

So.

What has happened in these two chapters?

[pause-2]

A letter has arrived.

And Raskolnikov has learned that his sister is about to marry a man she does not love—for his sake.

[pause-3]

The parallels Dostoevsky draws are unmistakable.

[pause-2]

Sonia, selling herself in the streets to feed her family.

Dounia, selling herself in marriage to secure her brother's future.

[pause-2]

Both are sacrifices.

Both are transactions.

Both turn women into commodities.

[pause-3]

And Raskolnikov sees this with terrible clarity.

[pause-2]

"Sonia's life is no worse than life with Mr. Luzhin," he thinks.

[pause-2]

He is not wrong.

Luzhin wants a wife who will be grateful.

Indebted.

Subordinate.

[pause-2]

This is not love.

It is purchase.

[pause-3]

But here is Raskolnikov's dilemma.

[pause-2]

He cannot stop the marriage because he has nothing to offer instead.

He is poor.

He is failing.

He cannot even pay his rent.

[pause-2]

What right does he have to forbid anything?

[pause-3]

And so his thoughts turn, again, to the idea.

The thing.

The plan.

[pause-2]

"I shall go to Razumihin's... on the next day after It."

"When It will be over and everything will begin afresh."

[pause-2]

He catches himself.

"But is It really going to happen?"

[pause-3]

Notice how Dostoevsky handles this.

[pause-2]

Raskolnikov never says what "It" is.

He cannot name it, even to himself.

The word "murder" never crosses his mind.

[pause-2]

Instead, there is only this vague, looming "It"—

This thing that will change everything.

That will give him power.

That will allow him to act instead of suffering.

[pause-3]

The letter from his mother is the catalyst.

[pause-2]

Before, the idea was a fantasy.

A game he played in his mind.

Something to pass the time in his coffin-room.

[pause-2]

Now, it has become necessary.

[pause-3]

Or so he tells himself.

[pause-2]

Because here is the other thing about Raskolnikov.

He needs to believe that he has no choice.

That circumstances are forcing him.

That he is a victim of fate.

[pause-2]

The poverty.

The sister's sacrifice.

The mother's borrowed roubles.

[pause-2]

He is assembling justifications.

Building a case.

[pause-3]

But the truth is simpler and darker.

[pause-2]

The idea was there first.

The theory came before the need.

[pause-2]

And now he is looking for reasons to do what he has already decided to do.

[pause-3]

Next time, a dream will show us what his conscious mind cannot admit.

A horse will be beaten to death.

And a chance encounter in the Hay Market will remove his last excuse for hesitation.

[pause-3]

Seven hundred and thirty steps.

The countdown has begun.

[pause-3]

Until then.
