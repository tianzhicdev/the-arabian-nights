Nietzsche: [settling in, curious] You wished to tell me about this book that was written against you.
Chernyshevsky: [picking up the book, weighing it in his hand] Dostoevsky published it in 1864, the year after my own novel appeared. [wry] He meant it as a refutation. A demonstration that my ideas about human perfectibility lead only to the underground—to paralysis, spite, and madness.
Nietzsche: [interested] And did he succeed?
Chernyshevsky: [setting the book down, thoughtful] He created something more disturbing than a refutation. He created a voice. [pause] Let me tell you what that voice says.
Chernyshevsky: [beginning, measured storytelling tone] The book opens with a confession. An unnamed man—we never learn his name—speaks directly to us. He is forty years old, a retired civil servant, living alone in a wretched room on the outskirts of St. Petersburg.
Nietzsche: [quietly] Against whom?
Chernyshevsky: [pointing] Exactly what he asks himself. [quoting] "I am well aware that I cannot 'pay out' the doctors by not consulting them; I know better than anyone that by all this I am only injuring myself and no one else. But still, if I don't consult a doctor, it is from spite. My liver is bad—well, let it get worse!"
Nietzsche: [thoughtful] He is making a principle of self-destruction.
Chernyshevsky: [agreeing] Yes. [continuing] He was a petty official—a collegiate assessor, one of the lowest ranks. He claims he was spiteful in his work, that he took pleasure in being rude to petitioners who came to his office. [sardonic] But even this is immediately undercut. He confesses he was never truly spiteful—that it was all performance. At the moment of his greatest apparent rage, he could be appeased with a cup of tea and a kind word.
Nietzsche: [recognizing something] He cannot commit to any identity.
Chernyshevsky: [emphatic] To anything! [quoting directly] "I could not become anything; neither spiteful nor kind, neither a rascal nor an honest man, neither a hero nor an insect." [explaining] This, he argues, is the inevitable fate of the intelligent man in the nineteenth century. Only fools become something definite. The truly conscious man is condemned to permanent irresolution.
Chernyshevsky: [shifting tone, more philosophical] Now he explains why. [building] His thesis—and Friedrich, this is aimed directly at everything I believe—his thesis is that consciousness itself is a disease. [quoting slowly, with weight] "I swear, gentlemen, that to be too conscious is an illness—a real thorough-going illness."
Nietzsche: [leaning forward, intense] Go on.
Chernyshevsky: [explaining] For ordinary life, he says, even a quarter of normal human consciousness would be too much. But he—a "cultivated man of our unhappy nineteenth century"—possesses consciousness in monstrous excess. And it has paralyzed him utterly.
Nietzsche: [disturbed but fascinated] The clarity becomes the trap.
Chernyshevsky: [nodding] And here is the most disturbing part. [building slowly] He discovers that even in this degradation—especially in this degradation—there is a secret enjoyment. [quoting] "I reached the point of feeling a sort of secret abnormal, despicable enjoyment in returning home to my corner on some disgusting Petersburg night, acutely conscious that that day I had committed a loathsome action again... and inwardly, secretly, gnawing, gnawing at myself for it, tearing and consuming myself till at last the bitterness turned into a sort of shameful accursed sweetness, and at last—into positive real enjoyment!"
Nietzsche: [slowly] He has discovered what I call *ressentiment*—turned entirely inward.
Chernyshevsky: [new section, building] He develops this into a theory of two types of men. [explaining] The first is what he calls the "direct" man, the man of action. This man is stupid but effective. When insulted, he feels revenge as simple justice. He charges toward his goal like—[quoting with sardonic edge] "an infuriated bull with its horns down."
Nietzsche: [understanding] The wall gives him permission to stop thinking.
Chernyshevsky: [agreeing] Exactly. [shifting] But the Underground Man is the opposite type. He is what he calls the "mouse"—the hyper-conscious man who cannot act. [describing] When insulted, he feels spite perhaps even more intensely than the man of action. But he cannot respond. His acute consciousness generates endless doubts and questions. [quoting] "One question leads to another; and the result is that what should be simple retaliation becomes complicated into a sort of fatal brew, a stinking mess, made up of doubts and emotions."
Nietzsche: [grim recognition] The wound becomes more precious than healing.
Chernyshevsky: [pointing at him] Yes. [continuing] He gives a concrete example. [quoting] "Even in toothache there is enjoyment." [explaining] The moans of an educated man with toothache are different from the moans of a peasant. The peasant moans innocently. But the educated man's moans are—[quoting] "malignant moans." They express the consciousness that there is no enemy to blame, only the impersonal laws of nature. And so he takes a perverse pleasure in annoying everyone around him, in displaying his suffering to an audience that secretly despises him.
Nietzsche: [darkly] Suffering becomes performance.
Chernyshevsky: [emphatic] Performance without possibility of action. [quoting] "The direct, legitimate fruit of consciousness is inertia—that is, conscious sitting-with-the-hands-folded."
Chernyshevsky: [shifting, new energy] Now. The attack on my philosophy begins in earnest.
Nietzsche: [alert] The Crystal Palace.
Chernyshevsky: [nodding grimly] The Crystal Palace. [explaining] In my novel, I described a future society organized on rational principles—where individual self-interest, properly understood, would lead naturally to collective harmony. The symbol for this was the Crystal Palace in London—that magnificent building of iron and glass. I saw it as proof that humanity could construct a perfect world through reason and cooperation.
Nietzsche: [appreciating] He wants the right to mock.
Chernyshevsky: [pressing on] More than that. [passionate] He attacks the very foundation of utilitarian ethics—the assumption that human beings, understanding their true interests, will naturally choose what benefits them. [quoting] "Shower upon man every earthly blessing, drown him in a sea of happiness, so that nothing but bubbles of bliss can be seen on the surface; give him economic prosperity, such that he should have nothing else to do but sleep, eat cakes, and busy himself with the continuation of his species, and even then—"
Nietzsche: [intense] He would burn down paradise to prove he is free.
Chernyshevsky: [confirming] To prove he is not a piano key! [quoting] "What man wants is simply independent choice, whatever that independence may cost and wherever it may lead." [continuing] Even if science could calculate human behavior with mathematical precision, even if they could prove that every action follows necessarily from prior causes—[quoting with fierce energy] "man would purposely go mad in order to be rid of reason and gain his point!"
Nietzsche: [slowly] Because suffering is consciousness. And consciousness, however painful, is life.
Chernyshevsky: [nodding] He puts it exactly this way. [quoting] "Twice two makes four is not life, gentlemen, but is the beginning of death." [pause] And then, most provocative of all: [quoting with dark amusement] "Twice two makes four seems to me simply a piece of insolence. Twice two makes four is a pert coxcomb who stands with arms akimbo barring your path and spitting. I admit that twice two makes four is an excellent thing, but if we are to give everything its due, twice two makes five is sometimes a very charming thing too."
Chernyshevsky: [shifting to narrative, new section] So much for the philosophy. Now he tells us a story—something from his past. He says a particular memory oppresses him and must be exorcised. [setting the scene] "The snow is falling," he writes, "almost wet snow."
Nietzsche: [quietly] The underground, before it became permanent.
Chernyshevsky: [agreeing] He was building it. [continuing] One night, seeking some distraction, he passes a tavern with a billiard room. Through the window, he sees a fight—a man thrown bodily out the window. [sardonic] He envies this man. At least something happened to him. At least he participated in life.
Nietzsche: [understanding] The insult that cannot be answered.
Chernyshevsky: [intense] He could have protested—and been thrown out the window himself. Instead, he did nothing. He left. [quoting] "I had been treated like a fly—insulted, humiliated, but my resentment died away; in my place some infuriated gentleman would have challenged him to a duel."
Nietzsche: [darkly amused] The revenge of the imagination.
Chernyshevsky: [building to climax] But finally—finally—he devises a plan. [explaining] On the Nevsky Prospect, where respectable people promenade, the Underground Man has noticed that he always steps aside for his betters. He gives way. But what if, when he encounters the officer, he refuses to yield? What if they collide as equals?
Nietzsche: [after a pause, quietly] He thinks this is victory.
Chernyshevsky: [heavily] He thinks this is victory. [pause] A year of planning, a small fortune spent on clothing, endless mental anguish—and the officer walks on without a flicker of recognition.
Nietzsche: [slowly] And you say this was written against your philosophy?
Chernyshevsky: [tired, honest] He believed that if my ideas triumphed, Russia would produce nothing but such men. [pause] Mice. Nursing their injuries in the dark. Preferring fantasy to any possible reality.
Nietzsche: [quietly] Perhaps he was right about the mice. [pause] But wrong about the cause.
Chernyshevsky: [looking at him] How so?
Nietzsche: [thoughtful] The Underground Man suffers not from too much rationality—but from too little power. [explaining] He cannot act not because he thinks too much, but because he has no avenue for action. No way to matter. [intense] Change that, and perhaps the consciousness becomes a tool rather than a trap.
Chernyshevsky: [considering] Perhaps. [pause] But wait until you hear what happens next. Wait until you see what he does when he finally encounters another human being who offers him a way out.
Nietzsche: [settling back] Tell me.
Chernyshevsky: [picking up the thread] Next time. The dinner party. Where everything begins to collapse.
