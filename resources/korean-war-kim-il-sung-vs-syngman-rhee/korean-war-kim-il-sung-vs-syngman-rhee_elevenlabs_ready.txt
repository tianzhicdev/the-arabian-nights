Kim Il-sung: [slowly, measuring his words] I did not expect you to come.

Syngman Rhee: [dry laugh] And I did not expect coffee. I expected bullets.

Kim Il-sung: The bullets will come later. For now... [sound of cup being set down] ...I wanted to see the man who sold our country to the Americans.

Syngman Rhee: [firmly] Sold? I saved it. I spent thirty years in Washington, in Hawaii, in Geneva—begging the world to remember that Korea existed. Where were you? Playing soldier in Manchuria?

Kim Il-sung: [coldly] I was fighting. The Japanese knew my name. They hunted me across the frozen forests of the north. While you were giving speeches in your nice American suit.

Syngman Rhee: [pause] The Japanese knew your name. [scoffs] They knew the name you stole. Kim Il-sung was a famous guerrilla. You took his legend like a coat.

Kim Il-sung: [dangerous quiet] Be careful, old man.

Syngman Rhee: I am seventy-five years old. I have been careful my whole life. I am tired of careful.

Kim Il-sung: [long pause, then softer] You are right that I am not the original Kim Il-sung. That man died fighting. I took the name to honor him. To become what he represented.

Syngman Rhee: [surprised] You admit this?

Kim Il-sung: To you? What does it matter? One of us will be dead within the year. The other will write the history.

Syngman Rhee: [sigh] On that, at least, we agree.

Kim Il-sung: [shifting tone, more conversational] Tell me something. You lived in America for decades. You have a doctorate from Princeton. You knew their presidents. What do you actually think of them?

Syngman Rhee: [measured] The Americans are... children. Powerful children with extraordinary toys. They genuinely believe they are liberating us. They cannot imagine that anyone might not want their version of freedom.

Kim Il-sung: [intrigued] This is not what I expected you to say.

Syngman Rhee: I am not their puppet, whatever your propaganda claims. I fight with them constantly. Truman despises me. MacArthur tolerates me because I am useful.

Kim Il-sung: [chuckles] And yet you need them. Without American soldiers, your government would have collapsed in three weeks.

Syngman Rhee: [bitter acknowledgment] Thirteen days. We nearly lost Seoul in thirteen days.

Kim Il-sung: [pause] That surprised me too. I thought you would hold longer.

Syngman Rhee: Your Soviet tanks surprised us. Where did a poor country get so many tanks?

Kim Il-sung: [evasive] We built them.

Syngman Rhee: [laughs] You built nothing. Stalin gave them to you. Just as Truman gave me nothing until it was almost too late.

Kim Il-sung: [defensive] Chairman Stalin supports the liberation of oppressed peoples.

Syngman Rhee: Chairman Stalin supports what is convenient for Russia. You are his pawn. I am Truman's pawn. The only difference is that I know it.

Kim Il-sung: [long silence, then quietly] You think I do not know?

Syngman Rhee: [surprised pause]

Kim Il-sung: [continuing, more openly] I went to Stalin three times. Three times. Begging permission to reunify our country. He said no. No. No. Then suddenly yes. Why?

Syngman Rhee: Because the Americans said Korea was outside their defense perimeter. Dean Acheson's speech.

Kim Il-sung: [nodding] January. Your own allies abandoned you with a speech.

Syngman Rhee: And then they changed their minds. That is America. They change their minds like the weather.

Kim Il-sung: [bitterly] Stalin calculated they would not intervene. He was wrong.

Syngman Rhee: And now Mao has sent three hundred thousand Chinese soldiers into our country.

Kim Il-sung: [pause] More than that.

Syngman Rhee: [stunned] More?

Kim Il-sung: Perhaps... [hesitating] ...half a million. Maybe more. I am not always told.

Syngman Rhee: [long exhale] Half a million Chinese. God help us.

Kim Il-sung: [quietly] God is not coming.

Syngman Rhee: [pause, then philosophical] Let me ask you something about China. You lived there. You fought alongside the Chinese communists. What do you think Mao actually wants?

Kim Il-sung: [thoughtfully] Mao wants what every Chinese emperor has wanted. A buffer between China and foreign powers. We are that buffer.

Syngman Rhee: So Korea means nothing to him except geography.

Kim Il-sung: [reluctantly] Korea means... [pause] ...Korea means we are the place where China and Japan and Russia and America collide. We have always been this place. For a thousand years.

Syngman Rhee: [reflective] My grandfather told me stories about the Japanese invasions of the sixteenth century. The Manchu invasions. The Mongols before that. We are the crossroads of empires.

Kim Il-sung: And now we are the crossroads of ideologies.

Syngman Rhee: [sardonic] An improvement?

Kim Il-sung: [bitter laugh] No.

Syngman Rhee: [pause] Tell me about the Chinese soldiers. The ones I have not met yet. What are they like?

Kim Il-sung: [reflective] They are... extraordinary. They have almost nothing. No air support. Limited artillery. Many of them have no winter clothing. But they keep coming. Wave after wave after wave.

Syngman Rhee: MacArthur thought he would be home by Christmas. He marched all the way to the Yalu River.

Kim Il-sung: [darkly satisfied] And then Peng Dehuai taught him something about war.

Syngman Rhee: [quietly] I lost many good men at the Chosin Reservoir. The marines barely escaped.

Kim Il-sung: The Chinese lost more. [pause] They always lose more. Human waves against machine guns.

Syngman Rhee: [disturbed] And this does not trouble you?

Kim Il-sung: [pause, then carefully] It... [long pause] It is necessary.

Syngman Rhee: [pressing] Necessary. Yes. We both say that word a great deal, don't we?

Kim Il-sung: [defensive] The revolution requires sacrifice.

Syngman Rhee: And democracy requires sacrifice. [bitter] We blow up bridges with our own people crossing them.

Kim Il-sung: [pause] The Han River bridge. I heard about that.

Syngman Rhee: [heavily] Eight hundred people. Maybe more. My own soldiers gave the order. They said it was necessary. To slow your tanks.

Kim Il-sung: [quietly] Did it slow them?

Syngman Rhee: [long pause] By a few hours. Maybe.

Kim Il-sung: [silence]

Syngman Rhee: You know what haunts me? The mathematics. Eight hundred lives for a few hours. What is the exchange rate? How many Korean lives equal one hour of tactical advantage?

Kim Il-sung: [uncomfortable] This is war. Commanders make decisions.

Syngman Rhee: And leaders sign the orders. Or don't sign them. Or look away while others sign them.

Kim Il-sung: [challenging] You think I am worse? Your police have killed thousands. The Jeju massacre. The Bodo League. You rounded up suspected communists and shot them.

Syngman Rhee: [coldly] Suspected traitors. During an invasion.

Kim Il-sung: [bitter] There it is. Suspected. You did not even know if they were guilty.

Syngman Rhee: [pause, then quietly] No. We did not always know.

Kim Il-sung: [pressing] And this does not trouble you?

Syngman Rhee: [long silence] Everything troubles me. I have not slept properly in six months. I close my eyes and I see Seoul burning. I see bodies in the streets. I see farmers who wanted nothing but rice and peace, caught between two armies who both claim to be liberating them.

Kim Il-sung: [surprised by the honesty] This is... not what your propaganda says.

Syngman Rhee: Your propaganda is not what you truly believe either. [pause] We are both performers. We say what we must say to keep our people fighting.

Kim Il-sung: [slowly] I believe in the revolution.

Syngman Rhee: Do you? Or do you believe in Kim Il-sung?

Kim Il-sung: [long pause] They are the same thing now.

Syngman Rhee: [quietly] Yes. That is the trap, isn't it? Once you become the symbol, you cannot separate yourself from the cause. If you fail, the cause fails.

Kim Il-sung: [reflective] When I was young... in the forests of Manchuria... I truly believed we were fighting for something pure. Korean independence. Freedom from Japan. But now...

Syngman Rhee: [prompting] Now?

Kim Il-sung: [sigh] Now I have Soviet advisors telling me which targets to bomb. Chinese generals commanding half my country. Stalin sends cables with instructions. Mao sends more cables with different instructions.

Syngman Rhee: [dry laugh] And I have MacArthur who thinks he is a god, and Truman who thinks he is a schoolteacher, and the United Nations which thinks it is a debating society.

Kim Il-sung: [chuckles despite himself] A debating society with tanks.

Syngman Rhee: [pause, then seriously] Let us talk about the bomb.

Kim Il-sung: [tensing] What about it?

Syngman Rhee: Truman has considered using it. On your side of the border. On China. MacArthur has requested it explicitly.

Kim Il-sung: [carefully] I am aware.

Syngman Rhee: The Soviets tested their own bomb last year. August. Stalin has atomic weapons now.

Kim Il-sung: [quietly] This changes everything. And nothing.

Syngman Rhee: Explain.

Kim Il-sung: [thoughtfully] Before... the Americans could threaten anyone. Now... they threaten and Stalin can threaten back. It is... what is the word... stalemate.

Syngman Rhee: [bitter] And we are in the middle. The place where the stalemate gets tested.

Kim Il-sung: Tested. Yes. That is a good word for what is happening to our country.

Syngman Rhee: I have seen the photographs from Hiroshima. From Nagasaki.

Kim Il-sung: [pause] I have seen them too.

Syngman Rhee: [slowly] One bomb. Seventy thousand people in a flash of light. Vanished. And then more dying for months afterward from the radiation.

Kim Il-sung: [quietly] The Japanese deserved it.

Syngman Rhee: [sharply] No one deserves that. Not even the Japanese.

Kim Il-sung: [long pause] You surprise me again, old man. After what they did to you. To our country.

Syngman Rhee: I spent three years in their prison. They tortured me. I lost friends to their execution squads. [pause] And I still say no one deserves that kind of death.

Kim Il-sung: [uncomfortable] You are too... Christian.

Syngman Rhee: [quiet laugh] Perhaps. Though I wonder what kind of Christian orders the deaths of thousands.

Kim Il-sung: The kind who survives.

Syngman Rhee: [pause] Yes. The kind who survives.

Kim Il-sung: [shifting topics] Tell me about the Japanese. You must have opinions. You spent your life fighting them.

Syngman Rhee: [thoughtfully] The Japanese are... disciplined. Methodical. They occupied us for thirty-five years and nearly erased our culture. They banned our language. Changed our names. Took our land. Used our women.

Kim Il-sung: [darkly] My mother could not speak Japanese. She refused to learn. The police beat her for speaking Korean in public.

Syngman Rhee: [pause] I did not know that.

Kim Il-sung: There are many things you do not know about me.

Syngman Rhee: [conceding] True. [pause] But now the Americans are rebuilding Japan. Turning our colonizers into their allies. MacArthur rules Tokyo like an emperor.

Kim Il-sung: [bitter] And Korean comfort women are forgotten. Korean laborers who died in Japanese mines are forgotten.

Syngman Rhee: This may be the only thing we truly agree on. The Americans have no memory. They cannot hold a grudge longer than a news cycle.

Kim Il-sung: [nodding] They fought Japan for four years and now Japan is their friend. They will fight us and then try to make us their friend too.

Syngman Rhee: [dry] If any of us survive.

Kim Il-sung: [pause, then curious] Do you think the Americans understand Asia at all?

Syngman Rhee: [long exhale] No. They see maps. They see containment lines. They see dominoes falling. They do not see people.

Kim Il-sung: [agreeing] They think China is just a larger Korea. They think Korea is just a smaller Japan.

Syngman Rhee: Acheson said Korea was a "peninsula pointing at Japan." That is how they see us. A piece of geography. A dagger.

Kim Il-sung: Or a shield.

Syngman Rhee: [bitter] Yes. A shield. Something to absorb the blows meant for someone else.

Kim Il-sung: [pause] And the Soviets are different?

Syngman Rhee: [sardonic] No. Stalin sees us as a distraction. Something to bleed America while he consolidates Europe.

Kim Il-sung: [defensive] The Soviet Union supports—

Syngman Rhee: [interrupting] The Soviet Union supports Soviet interests. Just as China supports Chinese interests. Just as America supports American interests. We are the only ones foolish enough to believe any of them care about Korea.

Kim Il-sung: [long silence] You are very cynical for a Christian.

Syngman Rhee: I am very old for a revolutionary. [pause] Age teaches cynicism.

Kim Il-sung: I am thirty-eight. What does that teach?

Syngman Rhee: [considering] Thirty-eight. You have lived through Japanese occupation, guerrilla war, Soviet occupation, civil war, world war. [pause] What does that teach you?

Kim Il-sung: [slowly] That the only way to survive is to become powerful. That weakness is death.

Syngman Rhee: [nodding] We agree on something else.

Kim Il-sung: But we disagree on how to become powerful.

Syngman Rhee: Yes. You believe power comes from the state. From collective will. From the workers united.

Kim Il-sung: And you believe power comes from... what? Voting? Money? American soldiers?

Syngman Rhee: [carefully] I believe power comes from legitimacy. From being chosen by your people.

Kim Il-sung: [scoffs] You were not chosen. You were installed by the Americans.

Syngman Rhee: I was elected. By the National Assembly.

Kim Il-sung: An assembly you controlled. After you eliminated your opponents.

Syngman Rhee: [pause] Kim Gu was not eliminated. He was assassinated by—

Kim Il-sung: [cutting in] By your man. Ahn Doo-hee. Everyone knows this.

Syngman Rhee: [long silence] Kim Gu was a patriot. His death was... regrettable.

Kim Il-sung: Regrettable. [bitter laugh] You sound like Stalin after the purges. "Regrettable excesses."

Syngman Rhee: [quietly] Perhaps all leaders sound the same in the end.

Kim Il-sung: [pause, then philosophical] Do you ever wonder if we are simply the weakest point? The tip of the spear where the great powers collide?

Syngman Rhee: [thoughtfully] Every day. Korea has always been this. The place where empires meet.

Kim Il-sung: China, Japan, Russia, America. They all want us. Or they want to deny us to each other.

Syngman Rhee: And we want... what? To exist? To be left alone?

Kim Il-sung: To be united. [pause] We both want that.

Syngman Rhee: Under different flags.

Kim Il-sung: Different flags. Same land. Same people.

Syngman Rhee: [heavily] The farmers do not care about flags. They care about rice.

Kim Il-sung: [quietly] I know.

Syngman Rhee: The women do not care about ideology. They care about their children.

Kim Il-sung: I know.

Syngman Rhee: And we send them to die anyway. For ideas they do not understand.

Kim Il-sung: [defensive] They understand freedom from exploitation.

Syngman Rhee: They understand a bullet kills you regardless of who fires it.

Kim Il-sung: [long pause] You asked earlier how much of a person's life is in their own hands.

Syngman Rhee: And?

Kim Il-sung: [slowly] I used to think... everything. That will and determination could overcome any obstacle. The guerrilla code. Survive by believing you will survive.

Syngman Rhee: And now?

Kim Il-sung: [sigh] Now I send men into battle knowing most will not return. I sign orders knowing they mean death for thousands. And I wonder... how much did those men choose? How much was chosen for them by history?

Syngman Rhee: [reflective] When I was young, I thought I could change everything through education. Through diplomacy. I went to Princeton. I met Woodrow Wilson. I thought I could convince the world to free Korea.

Kim Il-sung: And Wilson did nothing.

Syngman Rhee: Wilson said Korea was a Japanese matter. [bitter] A Japanese matter. As if we were property to be disposed of.

Kim Il-sung: So you learned that diplomacy was useless?

Syngman Rhee: I learned that the weak have no voice. That power speaks first and morality speaks later. [pause] Perhaps that made me harder than I should have been.

Kim Il-sung: [quietly] Perhaps that made both of us harder than we should have been.

Syngman Rhee: [pause, then changing tone] We should talk about what happens next.

Kim Il-sung: [cautious] What do you mean?

Syngman Rhee: The Chinese are pushing south again. MacArthur is falling back. Soon there will be another siege of Seoul.

Kim Il-sung: [pause] We will take the city back.

Syngman Rhee: For how long? Until the Americans counterattack again? Until they push back to the Yalu again? Until China sends another half million soldiers?

Kim Il-sung: [frustrated] What is your point?

Syngman Rhee: My point is that we are both trapped. Neither side can win. Not completely. Not permanently.

Kim Il-sung: [stubbornly] The revolution will triumph.

Syngman Rhee: Will it? Or will we fight back and forth across the 38th parallel for years? Destroying everything? Killing everyone?

Kim Il-sung: [long silence]

Syngman Rhee: Stalin does not care if Korea burns. Truman does not care if Korea burns. Mao does not care if Korea burns. Only we care. And we are busy burning it ourselves.

Kim Il-sung: [quietly] What would you have me do? Surrender?

Syngman Rhee: I would have us both stop pretending that our masters care about us. That we are fighting for anything except to be useful pawns in their game.

Kim Il-sung: [sharply] I am not a pawn.

Syngman Rhee: [wearily] We are all pawns, Kim Il-sung. Some pawns get promoted. Some get sacrificed. But we move where the hands that hold us decide.

Kim Il-sung: [pause, then bitter] You have become a philosopher in your old age.

Syngman Rhee: I have become tired. [pause] Do you know what I think about at night? When I cannot sleep?

Kim Il-sung: What?

Syngman Rhee: I think about a farmer I met once. Before the war. In a small village near Pusan. He was ancient. Maybe ninety years old. He had lived through the Donghak rebellion, the Japanese annexation, the colonial period, the liberation, the division.

Kim Il-sung: [listening]

Syngman Rhee: I asked him what he wanted for Korea. Do you know what he said?

Kim Il-sung: What?

Syngman Rhee: [pause] He said he wanted one harvest without soldiers marching through his fields. Just one. In his whole life, he had never had that.

Kim Il-sung: [silence]

Syngman Rhee: He died last year. Your soldiers shot him when they took his village. Or my soldiers shot him when we took it back. Nobody knows which. Nobody recorded his name.

Kim Il-sung: [quietly] One farmer.

Syngman Rhee: One farmer. Out of millions. [pause] Is that what the revolution is for? Is that what democracy is for? To kill old men who only wanted to harvest rice?

Kim Il-sung: [defensive but uncertain] Sacrifices must be—

Syngman Rhee: [interrupting, tired] Yes. I know. Sacrifices must be made. We have both made them. We will both make more. [pause] I just wonder sometimes if we have forgotten what we were sacrificing for.

Kim Il-sung: [long silence, then softly] I have not forgotten.

Syngman Rhee: Haven't you?

Kim Il-sung: [pause] I remember my father. He was a teacher. He believed in Korean independence. He died before he saw it.

Syngman Rhee: My father was a farmer. Like that old man. He wanted nothing but peace.

Kim Il-sung: [reflective] They would not recognize what we have become.

Syngman Rhee: [quietly] No. They would not.

Kim Il-sung: [pause, then shifting] The coffee is cold.

Syngman Rhee: [slight laugh] Yes. We have been talking too long.

Kim Il-sung: [standing] I should return. My generals will wonder where I am.

Syngman Rhee: [also standing] And my Americans will wonder what I am plotting.

Kim Il-sung: [pause] This conversation changes nothing.

Syngman Rhee: I know.

Kim Il-sung: Tomorrow we will be enemies again.

Syngman Rhee: We never stopped being enemies.

Kim Il-sung: [pause, then unexpectedly] I understand now why the Americans find you difficult.

Syngman Rhee: [wry] Because I refuse to be what they expect?

Kim Il-sung: Because you see too clearly. People who see clearly make their masters uncomfortable.

Syngman Rhee: [pause] The same could be said of you.

Kim Il-sung: [bitter smile] Stalin does not like questions. I have learned to stop asking them.

Syngman Rhee: That may be the saddest thing you have said today.

Kim Il-sung: [pausing] There is something else. Something I have wanted to ask.

Syngman Rhee: [sitting back down] What is it?

Kim Il-sung: [returning to his seat] You mentioned capitalism. Democracy. [pause] Do you truly believe in it? Or is it just... the flag you chose?

Syngman Rhee: [thoughtfully] When I was young, I believed absolutely. I read Adam Smith. John Locke. The American founders. I thought... if we could just have elections, property rights, individual liberty, Korea would flourish.

Kim Il-sung: And now?

Syngman Rhee: [sigh] Now I see that capitalism creates its own injustices. The landlords in my own country exploit the peasants. The factories pay starvation wages. The wealthy buy politicians.

Kim Il-sung: [leaning forward] This is what Marx wrote. This is why the workers must seize the means of production.

Syngman Rhee: [shaking head] And what happens then? The state becomes the landlord. The party becomes the factory owner. You replace many exploiters with one.

Kim Il-sung: [defensive] The party represents the people.

Syngman Rhee: Does it? [pause] You have purged how many rivals? How many party members have disappeared into your camps?

Kim Il-sung: [uncomfortable silence]

Syngman Rhee: [continuing] I have done the same. Different methods. Same result. Those who disagree with us vanish. Whether into prison or into the ground.

Kim Il-sung: [quietly] This is how power works.

Syngman Rhee: [nodding grimly] Yes. This is how power works. Under any flag.

Kim Il-sung: [pause, then curious] What do you think of Mao? Not as a communist. As a leader.

Syngman Rhee: [considering] Mao is... formidable. He unified China. A country that was fragmented for decades. Warlords, foreign occupiers, Japanese armies. He brought them all under one flag.

Kim Il-sung: He defeated Chiang Kai-shek. The Americans' man.

Syngman Rhee: [wry] Yes, he did. With peasants and willpower. Against American weapons and money.

Kim Il-sung: [proudly] This is the power of revolutionary consciousness.

Syngman Rhee: [skeptical] Or the power of promising land to hungry farmers. Mao did not win with ideology. He won by telling millions of poor peasants they could have the landlords' fields.

Kim Il-sung: [pause] That is... also true.

Syngman Rhee: And now those peasants are dying in Korea. By the tens of thousands. Frozen. Starving. Sent here to protect Chinese borders.

Kim Il-sung: [uncomfortable] They are volunteers.

Syngman Rhee: [sharp laugh] Volunteers. [pause] I have spoken with Chinese prisoners. They did not volunteer. They were told to go, and they went.

Kim Il-sung: [defensively] The imperialists must be stopped.

Syngman Rhee: The imperialists. [pause] Which ones? The Americans who bring food and medicine? Or the Chinese who bring bullets and demands?

Kim Il-sung: [angrily] The Americans bomb your own cities! Pyongyang is ruins!

Syngman Rhee: [heavily] And Seoul? Three times now. Captured, recaptured, destroyed, rebuilt, destroyed again. [pause] When this is over, will there be anything left to fight over?

Kim Il-sung: [long pause] The Americans have bombed every city in the north. Every factory. Every bridge. Every power plant.

Syngman Rhee: [quietly] I know.

Kim Il-sung: [bitter] They drop more bombs on us than they dropped on Japan. Do you understand? More bombs than the entire Pacific War.

Syngman Rhee: [pause] I did not know that.

Kim Il-sung: [passionate] They bomb hospitals. Schools. Dams. They call it strategic bombing. [scoffs] There is nothing strategic about burning children.

Syngman Rhee: [long silence] And your artillery shells? Your snipers? Are they more discriminate?

Kim Il-sung: [pause] No.

Syngman Rhee: [heavily] No. War does not discriminate. That is the one honest thing about it.

Kim Il-sung: [reflective] You know what the Chinese call Korea?

Syngman Rhee: What?

Kim Il-sung: Chaoxian. The land of morning calm. [bitter laugh] When was the last morning calm here?

Syngman Rhee: [sadly] Before I was born. Before my father was born.

Kim Il-sung: [pause] I remember one morning. In Manchuria. After a battle. The Japanese had retreated. My men were wounded, starving. But the sun rose over the mountains and everything was still. Perfectly still. No gunfire. No screaming. Just... silence.

Syngman Rhee: [quietly] What did you feel?

Kim Il-sung: [pause] For one moment... I felt Korean. Not communist, not guerrilla, not soldier. Just... a man from this land.

Syngman Rhee: [softly] I understand.

Kim Il-sung: [surprised] Do you?

Syngman Rhee: I spent thirty years away from Korea. In America, in Hawaii, in Europe. And every night, I dreamed of the mountains. The temples. The sound of the language.

Kim Il-sung: And when you returned?

Syngman Rhee: [pause] It was not the Korea I remembered. The Japanese had changed everything. And then we changed what they had not.

Kim Il-sung: [nodding] The country changes faster than the dream.

Syngman Rhee: Yes. We are both fighting for a Korea that no longer exists. Perhaps that never existed.

Kim Il-sung: [thoughtful] My grandmother used to tell stories about the Joseon dynasty. About the scholars and the kings. The time before Japan.

Syngman Rhee: [wry] Those scholars would not recognize either of us. A communist revolutionary. A Methodist president. We are both products of foreign ideas.

Kim Il-sung: [pause] Marx was German. Jesus was Jewish.

Syngman Rhee: [light laugh] And yet we kill each other in their names.

Kim Il-sung: [pause] Do you believe in God? Truly?

Syngman Rhee: [carefully] I believe... there must be something beyond this. Beyond the killing. Beyond the politics. Something that judges us.

Kim Il-sung: [skeptical] And when it judges you? For the Bodo League? For the massacres?

Syngman Rhee: [heavily] Then I will answer. I will not pretend I did nothing wrong.

Kim Il-sung: You think admitting guilt saves you?

Syngman Rhee: I think denying guilt damns us both. [pause] What will you say to Marx's judgment? To the tribunal of history?

Kim Il-sung: [long pause] I will say... I tried. I tried to free my country. The methods were harsh. The results were bloody. But the intention was pure.

Syngman Rhee: [quietly] Intentions. [pause] The road to hell.

Kim Il-sung: [defensive] You Christians and your hell.

Syngman Rhee: [pause] Look around us, Premier Kim. Look at the bodies. The burned villages. The orphaned children. [pause] If this is not hell, what is?

Kim Il-sung: [long silence]

Syngman Rhee: [continuing] I do not need to wait for divine judgment. We have created our own hell. Right here. On this peninsula. In this war that neither of us can stop.

Kim Il-sung: [quietly] We could stop it.

Syngman Rhee: Could we?

Kim Il-sung: [pause] If we agreed. Both of us. To end it. To tell our masters no more.

Syngman Rhee: [sad smile] And then what? Stalin replaces you. Truman replaces me. The war continues with different faces.

Kim Il-sung: [frustrated] Then what is the point? Of this conversation? Of anything?

Syngman Rhee: [pause] Perhaps there is no point. Perhaps we are just two men who wanted to understand each other before the end.

Kim Il-sung: [pause] The end?

Syngman Rhee: Of something. The war. Our lives. Our illusions. [pause] Something always ends.

Kim Il-sung: [standing again] You are too philosophical. Too resigned.

Syngman Rhee: And you are too young. Too certain that action changes things.

Kim Il-sung: Action is all we have.

Syngman Rhee: [nodding slowly] Perhaps. [pause] But words matter too. What we say to each other. How we remember.

Kim Il-sung: [at the door] No one will remember this conversation.

Syngman Rhee: [pause] We will.

Kim Il-sung: [long pause at the door] One question. Before I go.

Syngman Rhee: Ask it.

Kim Il-sung: If there were no Americans. No Soviets. No Chinese. Just Koreans. Could we have found another way?

Syngman Rhee: [long silence] I do not know. [pause] I want to say yes. I want to believe that we could have built something together. But...

Kim Il-sung: But we are too different.

Syngman Rhee: No. [pause] I think we are too similar. Both too proud. Both too certain. Both too willing to sacrifice others for what we believe.

Kim Il-sung: [quietly] Perhaps that is the tragedy.

Syngman Rhee: [nodding slowly] Perhaps.

Kim Il-sung: [opening the door] Goodbye, President Rhee.

Syngman Rhee: Goodbye, Premier Kim.

Kim Il-sung: [pause at threshold] When this is over... when they write the history...

Syngman Rhee: Yes?

Kim Il-sung: [softly] I hope they remember that we both loved Korea. Even as we destroyed it.

Syngman Rhee: [pause] They will not remember that. [pause] They will remember the flags. The speeches. The battles. They will not remember two tired men drinking cold coffee, wondering where it all went wrong.

Kim Il-sung: [long pause] Then perhaps it is good that we had this one hour. Even if no one else knows.

Syngman Rhee: [quietly] Perhaps.

Kim Il-sung: [final pause, then leaving]

Syngman Rhee: [alone, murmuring to himself] One harvest without soldiers. [long pause, sigh] One harvest. [pause] Forgive us, old man. Whoever you were. We could not even give you that.
