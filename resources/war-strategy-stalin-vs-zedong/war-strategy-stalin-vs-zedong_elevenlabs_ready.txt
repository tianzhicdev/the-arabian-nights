Stalin: [leaning forward, cold] So. The peasant revolutionary finally sits at the table. Tell me, Mao—when you were hiding in your caves in Yan'an, did you ever imagine you'd be lecturing me about war?

Mao: [sharp, defensive] Those caves kept us alive while your advisors told us to surrender to Chiang Kai-shek. [bitter laugh] We won because we ignored Moscow's instructions.

Stalin: [cutting in] You won because I gave you Manchuria's industrial base in 1945. [pause] Don't confuse survival with strategy.

Mao: [pressing] Strategy? You signed a pact with Hitler. You purged your best generals before the Nazis invaded. [quieter, pointed] I read about Tukhachevsky. Brilliant man. You shot him in 1937.

Stalin: [voice tight, dangerous] Tukhachevsky was a traitor. [WHISPER] And I won the war anyway. Stalingrad. Berlin. [building] Where was your Long March when real empires were burning?

Mao: [calm, philosophical] The Long March taught us what you never learned—war is about the people. [leaning in] You moved factories beyond the Urals. I moved the revolution into every village. Your soldiers fought for fear. Mine fought because they believed.

Stalin: [dark laugh] Believed? [mocking] You redistributed land and called it ideology. I industrialized a feudal wasteland in ten years. [matter-of-fact] Your peasants with rifles—they're useful. But rifles don't build steel mills.

Mao: [fierce] Steel mills don't win guerrilla wars. [pause] You want to know the difference between us? You needed twenty million dead to stop the Wehrmacht. We defeated the Kuomintang—better armed, better supplied—because we understood the terrain was human.

Stalin: [interrupting, cold] Twenty-seven million. [silence] Get the number right if you're going to use my dead as a talking point.

Mao: [softer, caught off guard] I—

Stalin: [relentless] You talk about the people. [building intensity] I *was* the people. Factory workers. Peasants who couldn't read. [SHOUTING] I turned them into an industrial power while you were writing poetry in Hunan!

Mao: [recovering, steady] And what did it cost you? [probing] The famines in Ukraine. The purges. [pause] Your own son died in a German camp because you refused to negotiate his exchange.

Stalin: [barely audible, dangerous] You will not speak of Yakov.

Mao: [pressing on] I lost Yang Kaihui to the Nationalists in 1930. They executed her. [voice tight] But I didn't sacrifice my revolution's principles to save her. You sacrificed *people* for... what? Control?

Stalin: [explosive] CONTROL IS SURVIVAL! [breathing hard, then quieter] You think your caves taught you about war? [bitter] War is production. Logistics. [counting off] Tanks, planes, artillery—do you know how many T-34s we built? Fifty-seven thousand. How many did you build?

Mao: [calm, almost amused] None. [pause] We captured Chiang's American tanks and turned them against him. [philosophical] That's the essence of strategy—using the enemy's strength as his weakness.

Stalin: [dismissive] That's the essence of poverty. [leaning back] You're describing necessity as virtue.

Mao: [sharp] And you're describing brutality as efficiency. [pause] Tell me—when the Germans reached Moscow in 1941, what saved you? Your Five-Year Plans? [quieter] Or the fact that Russian winter and Russian bodies bought you time to move those factories?

Stalin: [cold, precise] Both. [matter-of-fact] I used every advantage. Geography. Weather. [pause] The willingness to sacrifice. [pointed] You did the same on the Long March—what were your casualty rates? Seventy percent?

Mao: [admitting] Close. [quiet] But the thirty thousand who survived became the core of everything. [building] Quality over quantity. Your army in 1941—you'd purged the officers who could actually think. We promoted the ones who could.

Stalin: [venomous] My army took Berlin. [pause] Where's your Berlin, Mao? Taiwan? [mocking] Still waiting for that final victory?

Mao: [stung, recovering] We'll take Taiwan when the time is right. [defensive] Unlike you, I don't need to conquer neighbors to prove the revolution worked.

Stalin: [bitter laugh] Eastern Europe isn't conquest—it's a buffer. [intense] You want to know what I learned from Hitler's invasion? [WHISPER] Never again. [building] Every satellite state is insurance. Every tank division is a wall between Moscow and the next Wehrmacht.

Mao: [softer, probing] And yet you're still afraid. [pause] I saw it in Moscow last year. December 1949. You kept me waiting for weeks. Negotiating that pact—[shaking head]—you treated me like a subordinate.

Stalin: [cutting] You were asking for money.

Mao: [fierce] I was asking for partnership! [pause, quieter] Three hundred million dollars. With interest. [bitter] That's what the Soviet Union offers its greatest ally? The Americans gave Chiang billions.

Stalin: [cold] The Americans lost. [pause] You're welcome.

Mao: [long pause, then quiet] You know what I realized in Moscow? [vulnerable] You don't see me as an equal. After everything—the Long March, the Japanese, defeating the Nationalists—you still see a peasant from Hunan.

Stalin: [silence, then honest] I see someone who hasn't been tested by total war. [matter-of-fact] You fought guerrillas and warlords. I fought industrialized genocide. [pause] They're not the same.

Mao: [challenging] Korea will be my test, then. When Kim invades—

Stalin: [sharp] *If* you support him. [pointed] That's why I need you committed, Mao. The Americans can't fight China directly. You're my safety belt.

Mao: [realizing] You're using me. [pause] You'll support Kim's invasion, but you want Chinese blood to stop the Americans, not Soviet.

Stalin: [unapologetic] I'm using strategy. [leaning in] You want to prove you're my equal? Win a war against the West. [pause] Then we'll talk about partnership.

Mao: [quiet, bitter] And if we lose?

Stalin: [cold] Then you weren't my equal after all.
